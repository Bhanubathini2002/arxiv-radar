### [Pix2Evidence: A Multimodal RAG System for Text and Visual Evidence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41335)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.41335

> Presents a multimodal RAG system, highly relevant for document retrieval and evidence grounding.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over visually rich documents must find both relevant content and the evidence that supports an answer. Screenshot retrieval preserves images and page structure, but written facts within screenshots also need to be searchable. Moreover, topical relevance alone does not identify which retrieved tile contains evidence for the answer. We introduce Pix2Evidence, a multimodal RAG system that encodes each screenshot tile together with its available transcription into a single fused image--text representation, so that layout and readable text are considered jointly during retrieval. To guide evidence selection, a vision-language teacher describes the visible content that supports each training question. We use these descriptions to adapt the retriever, then rerank the retrieved candidates before answer generation. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Pix2Evidence outperforms state-of-the-art methods. Across five widely-used Wikipedia QA benchmarks, the non-reranked Pix2Evidence leads the main comparison in answer accuracy on four. On MMSearch, it reaches 26.0\%, 4.4 percentage points above PixelRAG. We will release the code and share derived data where licensing and privacy reviews permit.

</details>

### [CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42054)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.42054

> Directly addresses causality and faithfulness in reasoning, critical for grounded and faithful RAG generation.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become the standard method for training language models to reason. However, RLVR typically relies solely on outcome correctness, which does not guarantee that the CoT causally contributes to the final answer. Without this connection, outcome rewards may fail to reinforce high-quality reasoning. Existing causality metrics assess CoT causality by perturbing the CoT, such as deleting reasoning steps or injecting errors, and measuring changes in the final answer. However, these perturbations inevitably alter information in each step. Consequently, high causality scores may reflect sensitivity to information changes introduced by perturbation rather than true causality. Such confounding effects further induce undesirable reasoning behaviors when these metrics are used as training rewards, including generating less informative CoTs and reducing exploration and self-correction. To address this problem, we propose CRoS (Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs), a novel CoT causality metric that perturbs the order of reasoning steps without altering content, thereby preserving information within each step. On Reasoning Gym with Gemma-3-4B, CRoS outperforms existing causality and outcome rewards in out-of-domain accuracy, CoT quality, and CoT faithfulness. Specifically, CRoS achieves 46.8% accuracy on five unseen logic and games datasets, outperforming the strongest baseline (43.8%). It also achieves better CoT quality, consistently outperforming baselines in CoT-Pass@K from K=1 to 128, averaging 43.4% vs. 41.4%. For CoT faithfulness, CRoS achieves a hint acknowledgment score of 34.0% vs. 20.2%.

</details>

### [Enhancing Knowledge Entity Relation Extraction in Abstracts and Full-text Content of Academic Articles Using Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41552)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.41552

> Directly examines knowledge entity relation extraction from academic papers, aligning well with document ingestion for RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

This study investigates the effectiveness of large pre-trained language models (LLMs) for knowledge entity relation extraction (KERE) from academic papers. Existing studies have examined individual types of LLM-based strategies and evaluated model performance using metrics such as precision and recall. To provide a broader evaluation, this study investigates multiple strategies, including closed-source LLM-based CoT prompting, LoRA-adapted open-source LLMs, LLM-enhanced small pre-trained language models (SLMs), and SLM-enhanced closed-source LLMs. In addition, this study introduces two strategies, namely SLM-enhanced open-source LLMs and closed-source LLM-enhanced open-source LLMs. These strategies are evaluated from multiple perspectives, including metrics, their ability to mitigate errors observed in SLMs, and potential directions for model optimization. This study manually constructs two annotated datasets in the NLP domain, covering Abstract and Full-Text datasets. Experimental results show that the LoRA-adapted T5 model achieves the highest macro F1 scores of 0.7408 and 0.7013 on the Abstract and Full-Text datasets, respectively. SLMs also benefit from LLM-generated entity descriptions under the LLM-enhanced SLM strategy. Error analysis further reveals that the effectiveness of LLM-based strategies in mitigating SLM errors varies across error types. Based on the identified limitations and error patterns of these strategies, several optimization directions are proposed. In addition, combining strategies with different error patterns further improves KERE performance.

### [KEEPER: Learning Control-Sufficient Textual Memory for Long-Horizon LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41482)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.41482

> The paper discusses long-term memory for LLM agents, highly relevant to conversational memory management in chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Recursive textual memory can carry earlier errors into later updates, yet task performance alone does not establish memory reliability. We define self-consistency bias as the persistence of incorrect claims already present in the incoming memory. We present KEEPER, a comparative analysis of four auxiliary objectives along information-content and update-sensitivity axes within a shared recursive interface, examining how supervision targets relate to agent performance. Among evaluated conditions, reward-related prediction achieve the highest task performance before and after offline reinforcement learning, outperforming joint supervision. A claim-level analysis of one baseline trajectory documents error persistence despite corrective observations. Together, these analyses motivate evaluating error persistence and valid-information retention alongside task outcomes.

### [Context as Infrastructure: Knowledge Compilation for Retrieval in Agentic Systems with Harnesses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41172)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.41172

> Directly addresses knowledge compilation for retrieval in agentic systems, essential for RAG architectures.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieval-augmented generation is becoming agentic. A search harness writes queries, reads results, reformulates, and decides when to stop. This largely solves accuracy: a harness with only BM25/grep and MiniMax M3 answers 85% of BrowseComp-Plus and 91% on multi-hop FRAMES in our runs, without compiled or vector indexes. It exposes a context problem: an undisciplined loop repeats queries, rereads documents, and fills the window with unused passages. The missing layer is knowledge compilation: a configurable ingestion pipeline where LLMs enrich documents with context no chunk contains alone--navigation hierarchies with claim-evidence leaves, entity graphs, timelines, mind maps, ontologies, wikis, and memory. Compilation is not a better retriever: under a competent harness, structural artifacts gave no detectable accuracy gain on FRAMES, and a PageIndex-style outline degraded at scale. It is a supply layer. A navigation tree whose leaves are claim-evidence pairs--the mechanism we measure--cuts query tokens by 91% when evidence suffices and adds 1.9% when it does not, a 38% average reduction; this implies a 2% break-even sufficiency rate and a measured rate near 43%. A decision gate cuts latency from 140 s to 30 s, though substitution's token saving is bought with retrieval rounds and carries its own latency cost. Other artifacts--ontology, graph, wiki, timeline, mind map, memory--pay off in deployment (governance, integration, onboarding, temporal orientation, continuity) where accuracy benchmarks cannot see them. We describe RAGFlow's pipeline and harness (open source at https://github.com/infiniflow/ragflow), which turns the loop into five disciplined stations and keeps the context entering the model the smallest sufficient set; together they form an agent-native database: a store that hands the agent compiled, governed context instead of a ranked list of passages.

</details>

### [Language Models Show Conformity Bias in RAG: When the Majority Endorses a Poisoned Claim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41012)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.41012

> This paper investigates conformity bias in RAG, directly relevant to quality assurance in retrieval.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can expose language models to misleading evidence in retrieved documents. We investigate whether increasing the number of mutually agreeing poisoned documents affects a model's ability to detect misinformation. We evaluate 200 randomly selected questions from SciFact (random seed 38), comparing contexts with one poisoned and six healthy documents against contexts with six mutually agreeing poisoned documents and one healthy document. For Gemma-3-12b, poison-detection rates decrease from 25% (50/200) in the minority condition to 10% (20/200) in the majority condition. Among the 38 questions with different outcomes across conditions, 34 change from detected to undetected, while only four change in the opposite direction (exact McNemar test, p = 6.04e-7). This pattern is consistent with a conformity-like response to a majority of mutually agreeing poisoned documents. Our results provide initial evidence of conformity bias in RAG-based misinformation detection: Gemma-3-12b is substantially less likely to detect poisoned claims when they dominate the retrieved evidence.

</details>

### [KITE: Retrieval as a Decision Procedure for Long-Term Conversational Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41521)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.41521

> Introduces a framework for long-term conversational memory that aligns well with RAG principles.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Many long-term memory systems for LLM agents are built around embedding-based retrieval. Al- though effective for semantic matching, similarity ranking alone does not establish whether re- trieved memories satisfy the conditions implied by a question. In this paper, we introduce KITE (Knowledge-Indexed Temporal Evidence), a vector-free framework that formulates memory re- trieval as an explicit decision procedure. KITE organizes multi-session conversations as structured knowledge indexed by a governed topic vocabulary, which evolves through controlled expansion and consolidation. At query time, questions are compiled into inspectable retrieval plans over the same knowledge index that guides memory construction. The plans are then executed through fixed lookup and ranking operations, while sparse lexical retrieval recovers surface details omitted from the structured representation. Experiments on LoCoMo and LongMemEval-S show that KITE achieves 93.5% and 86.0% accuracy while using only about 1.6k reader-context tokens per ques- tion. These results demonstrate that conversational memory can move beyond similarity ranking by unifying memory construction and query compilation around a governed index, turning retrieval into an inspectable decision procedure for determining which evidence satisfies the question.

</details>

### [Defining Page-Centric Visual Document Retrieval and Evaluating Existing Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41360)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.41360

> Defines page-centric retrieval which is crucial for document chunking strategies in RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

In modern information retrieval (IR), documents are increasingly encountered as structured sequences of visually rich, paginated units, rather than as flat text. Example documents encountered in our daily life include Word files, PDFs, and slide decks. Free text-centric retrieval systems either ignore this structure entirely or destroy it through arbitrary passage chunking. In this paper, we formulate page-centric Visual Document Retrieval (VDR) with pages as the fundamental indexing units and document context available for assessing page relevance. The formulation distinguishes two retrieval granularities, page and document, and three retrieval paradigms: independent-item, evidence-set, and iterative retrieval. We then assess 42 benchmarks using eight criteria covering corpus characteristics, query construction, relevance annotation, and ranking support. Our analysis shows two main findings on current VDR research. First, evaluation remains concentrated on page-level independent-item retrieval, with fewer resources supporting document-level, evidence-set, and iterative settings. Second, queries originating from real user information needs independently of target corpus content and graded relevance judgments are uncommon, while the coverage and verification of relevance judgments vary substantially. Both findings reveal gaps between the capabilities applications require and those existing evaluation protocols can establish. We provide benchmark selection guidance and identify priorities for future benchmark design, emphasizing alignment between task requirements, relevance semantics, and evaluation protocols.

</details>

### [Prevented is Not Protected: What Attack Success Rate Hides about RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40742)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.40742

> Addresses vulnerabilities in RAG systems, critical for understanding safety and reliability in production.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is vulnerable to knowledge poisoning, where adversaries manipulate retrieved evidence to influence model outputs. Existing defenses are primarily evaluated using attack success rate (ASR), which obscures defense-induced failures and the mechanisms behind apparent attack prevention. To address this issue, this work introduces \textbf{Aligned RAG Evaluation}, a matched-query protocol that jointly examines clean and poisoned responses, with and without defense. By combining matched conditions analysis, LLM-judge endorsement, and retrieval dependence controls, the proposed framework distinguishes prevented from introduced failures, target mentions from LLM endorsement, and single-passage influence from evidence composition. Experiments on HotpotQA and Natural Questions reveal that existing defenses can introduce unexpected target-positive answers, although they can prevent some attacks, while evaluation criteria can also change defense rankings. Our findings expose critical blind spots in ASR-based evaluation and motivate a more comprehensive assessment of RAG poisoning defenses. The code and the evaluation artifact are available at https://github.com/chxhana/aligned-rag-eval.

</details>

### [DecisionRank: On-Policy Distillation for Decision-Model Rerankers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42128)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.42128

> Relevant to reranking in decision models, which can impact RAG quality and retrieval tasks.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Decision models target structured tasks by directly predicting choices or scores, avoiding autoregressive decoding latency. However, transferring a generative LLM's ranking knowledge to a decision model remains challenging: the teacher expresses preferences through a ranked list of document ID sequences, while the student determines the ranking using scores from the first-token position. To bridge this gap, we introduce DecisionRank, a decision model trained through item-space on-policy distillation (OPD). Item-space OPD constructs the student's rollout by sorting its document scores into a ranked sequence. A generative teacher takes the resulting ranking list as inputs and provides conditional feedback to directly supervise those scores. Combined with offline ranking supervision, DecisionRank achieves state-of-the-art performance on both general and reasoning-intensive retrieval benchmarks such as TREC DL, BEIR, and BRIGHT. Across four benchmarks, item-space OPD consistently outperforms alternative training objectives. Even at 1.7B parameters, DecisionRank surpasses its 27B generative teacher on TREC DL19--23. At 8B, it outperforms its generative listwise counterpart while running $24.5\times$ faster on TREC DL and $16.7\times$ faster on BRIGHT.

</details>

### [Completeness and Cost of Retrieval Structures for Multi-Tool Requests in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41905)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.41905

> Discusses retrieval structures relevant to multi-tool agent queries, pertinent for RAG design.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

An agent that must call several tools to fulfill a request fails when the tool retriever omits any one of them, yet tool retrievers are mostly built and evaluated on queries requiring a single tool. We compare representatives of six retrieval structures (flat lexical and dense retrieval, LLM query decomposition, LLM-navigated hierarchies, server-then-tool matching, dependency graphs, and trained set-level retrievers) across two multi-tool testbeds: 345 AppWorld queries with expert dependency labels and 103 MCP-Bench tasks over 257 MCP tools. We measure completeness@k, the share of queries whose entire required tool set appears in the top k, alongside the LLM calls, tokens, and latency that each structure incurs at retrieval time. A motivating analysis on all 7,961 ToolRet queries shows that aggregate completeness mixes two regimes: completeness at 10 is .58 to .67 on single-target queries and .08 to .17 on queries with four or more targets, and the ordering of retrievers on single-target queries reverses on multi-target ones. Across the two testbeds, none of the compared structures that is untrained on the testbed and makes at most one LLM call per query completes more than 10% of AppWorld queries at k = 16. The two structures that do better incur different costs: a set-level retriever trained on 75 to 90 in-domain queries reaches .33 on AppWorld and .58 on MCP-Bench with no LLM calls, while an LLM-navigated hierarchy reaches .30 and .40 with a median of 31 to 38 LLM calls and about five minutes per query. Their failure modes also differ: the trained retriever mostly ranks the missing tools just below the cutoff, whereas the hierarchy finds the target API and stops before collecting its prerequisites. In a downstream check with the official AppWorld agent, tasks with a complete retrieved set succeeded 21 of 21 times, whereas incomplete ones succeeded 2 of 39 times.

### [When Steering Shifts Discourse: Identifying and Intervening on Discourse-Level Changes due to Activation Steering in LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41661)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.41661

> Addresses discourse-level changes and output tuning, relevant for guardrails and safety in LLM applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Activation steering is an inference-time technique used to control or modify the behavior, tone, or safety properties of a large language model (LLM) without retraining the model's weights or editing its input prompt, but simply by adding a vector to its residual-stream activations. However, activation steering also changes the generated text in unintended ways, unrelated to intended behavior change, that are not well understood. Prior work has measured these changes only through coarse signals such as perplexity. In this work, we show that steering systematically produces significant changes in discourse dimensions of the generated text, such as its formality, use of evidence, and ambiguity. We present a way of countering this by adding a second, complementary vector that targets the affected discourse dimension. This intervention restores about 78% of the unintended discourse changes while preserving the intended steering behavior in about 93% of the generations.

### [In-Model Context Compression with Agentic Soft Tokens for Long-Horizon LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41291)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.41291

> Introduces context management strategies which are relevant for conversational memory in chat assistants.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Long-horizon LLM agents play an important role in solving complex and sustained tasks, with ever-growing interaction contexts. However, these accumulated contexts will raise substantial computational cost and obscure key decision-relevant evidence. Existing methods typically train external compressors or context-management agents to rewrite context as shorter hard-token sequences, but such external selection may not align with the LLM's evolving representation and is necessarily lossy. In this paper, we introduce Agentic Soft-Token Compression (ASTC), a training-free compression method using soft tokens within LLM agent for long-horizon tasks. Specifically, ASTC uses the model's native recurrent dynamics to determine the already absorbed information, leveraging the commonly-used hybrid Gated Delta Network (GDN)-attention. It then carries this information forward as agentic soft tokens while retaining exact values as hard tokens. The resulting compact sequence reduces later-layer computation and yields reusable KV caches, extending the savings across the agent's trajectory. Extensive experiments on long-horizon tasks show that ASTC matches or exceeds the uncompressed agent's accuracy with up to 50.68% fewer end-to-end Attention Pairs, while trained compressors require 15.4 to 103.7 times more Attention Pairs per successful task. These findings highlight agentic soft tokens as a model-native way to carry absorbed context forward as the task representation evolves.

### [Better Retrievers, Worse Labels: How Incomplete Relevance Judgments Distort Abstention Evaluation in Conversational RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40966)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.40966

> Addressing evaluation methods in conversational RAG, it touches on retrieval and answerability metrics.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Conversational RAG benchmarks such as MTRAG and RETECO evaluate retrieval and generation end to end: each system answers from its own retrieval, so abstention must be scored against whether each retrieved context is sufficient. A convenient shortcut reads sufficiency off the relevance judgments and treats a context without gold documents as unanswerable. We re-run the official baselines exactly and test this shortcut on RECOR (RETECO track~2) with four retrievers (nDCG@10 from .18 to .59) and three Qwen3 readers. Two open LLM judges and a panel of four small open models label each context by content, and a model of another family audits 250 contexts blind, with adjudication. The stronger the retriever, the larger the share of no-gold contexts that still contain the answer (28\% to 72\%) and the lower the shortcut's precision (.68 to .10; .70 to .21 under the audit). A reader agrees: Qwen3-4B answers 62--67\% of these false misses correctly, against 23--30\% of no-gold contexts judged insufficient and 1\% with no documents. In most false misses, the answer comes from a hard negative mined for the same question, another turn's gold or a neighbouring chunk of the gold page. On MTRAG, whose judgments come from annotators, precision drops from .72 to .50. Under the shortcut, answerability AUROC is up to .19 too low, and the best sufficiency detector appears to be a retrieval score that never reads the documents. We release code, runs and labels, and recommend scoring refusals against content labels, not the presence of gold.

</details>

### [Search When Needed, Proceed When Reliable: Metacognitive Reliability Supervision for Efficient Agentic Search](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41738)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.41738

> Proposes a metacognitive approach to improve search decision-making in agents, relevant for grounding answers in documents.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Efficient agentic search requires large language models (LLMs) to determine whether they can reliably proceed with their current knowledge or need additional external evidence. Supervision based on final-answer correctness or trajectory-level feedback evaluates realized trajectory performance. However, observed trajectory performance is jointly shaped by stochastic generation, retrieval quality, and downstream reasoning. Such signals therefore provide only indirect evidence of how reliable the current decision state was and how retrieved evidence changed its reliability. An effective reward should encourage search when external information is needed and favor searches that contribute to the answer. To this end, we propose a metacognitive framework for training LLMs to make search decisions based on the reliability of their intermediate reasoning states. Our framework estimates the reliability of local conclusions to distinguish whether the current information supports proceeding without search from how much retrieved evidence improves that reliability. The current-state reliability score assesses whether the model can proceed without search. The search-action score measures how retrieved evidence changes estimated reliability. Together, these scores define a reliability-aware process reward for Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), providing finer-grained supervision for search and no-search behavior. Experiments across seven question-answering benchmarks demonstrate that our method achieves a better accuracy-efficiency trade-off than strong agentic-search baselines. Our code and model checkpoints will be open-sourced.

</details>

### [Hierarchical Dual-Graph Retrieval for Long-form Audio Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41390)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.41390

> Introduces a RAG framework for audio reasoning, relevant to multi-hop retrieval techniques.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Large audio language models (LALMs) struggle with long-form audio reasoning, as processing entire recordings often limits to capture query-relevant content distributed across distant segments while incurring substantial computational cost. We introduce AudioHelix, a hierarchical DualGraph framework tailored for LALMs' retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. Unlike existing text-only or non-graph RAG approaches, AudioHelix connects related textual and acoustic evidence across temporally distant segments for multi-hop reasoning. Extensive experiments on TraceAV-Bench, MMAU-Pro, and AudioMarathon demonstrate that AudioHelix consistently outperforms full-audio processing and existing RAG methods. The project site is https://github.com/ewha-mmai/audiohelix.

</details>
