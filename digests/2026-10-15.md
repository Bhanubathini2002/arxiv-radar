### [Retrieve What Helps, Only When It Helps: Agentic Reinforcement Learning for Selective Retrieval-Augmented Machine Translation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19528)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.19528

> Introduces a reinforcement learning framework specifically for selective retrieval in machine translation, highly relevant for RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieving external knowledge can improve large language model (LLM) translation by providing supplementary information such as idiom interpretations and terminology. However, existing retrieval-augmented machine translation systems typically apply predefined retrieval strategies uniformly across all inputs. We argue that translation operates in a partial-evidence regime: LLM's parametric knowledge suffices for many inputs, while external evidence benefits a subset and usually supports rather than directly provides the desired translation. This creates two challenges: deciding when to retrieve and what evidence to acquire. We introduce Selective Retrieval-Augmented Translation (SRAT), an agentic reinforcement learning framework that learns when to translate directly or retrieve, what to retrieve, and how to integrate these decisions by optimizing final translation quality. To align evidence retrieval with downstream translation quality rather than simple semantic similarity, we further develop joint Plackett--Luce optimization and translation-quality contrastive training for retriever-training. Experiments on idiom, knowledge-intensive, and document-level translation with offline or online retrieval show that SRAT consistently outperforms never- and always-retrieve baselines, and quality-aligned retriever training provides further gains when similarity-based retrieval is insufficient. The results demonstrate the benefit of treating retrieval as a learnable translation action rather than fixed preprocessing.

### [ARMED: Adaptive Retrieval-Augmented Medication Recommendation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16440)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.16440

> Directly presents a novel RAG framework relevant to medication recommendations, a key application.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Medication recommendation requires jointly considering diagnoses, comorbidities, longitudinal patient history, and treatment interactions. Although recent retrieval-augmented generation approaches using language models have shown promise for clinical recommendation, most existing systems rely on fixed retrieval pipelines that cannot adapt to diverse clinical retrieval scenarios and may introduce noisy or irrelevant evidence. We propose ARMED, an adaptive clinical RAG framework that dynamically expands retrieval according to retrieval sufficiency and current-admission relevance. ARMED first observes the initial retrieval outcome and selectively performs supplementary or fallback multi-query retrieval when additional evidence is needed. The framework also incorporates a relevance gate that activates only clinically relevant same-patient history and organizes retrieved evidence into structured query-level clinical evidence. Experiments on MIMIC-IV demonstrate that ARMED consistently outperforms previous static retrieval baselines and improves overall medication recommendation quality. By combining locally runnable small language models with adaptive retrieval, ARMED supports medication recommendation without relying on external language model APIs or task-specific fine-tuning.

### [Retrieving Complementary Evidence for Multi-Hop RAG with Semantic Gain Graphs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15285)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.15285

> Directly addresses multi-hop retrieval in RAG, contributing to grounded generation strategies.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multi-hop retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) requires finding documents whose evidence is useful in combination. Query relevance and graph connectivity help locate related content, but neither directly measures the additional information one document contributes given another. We introduce SG$^2$RAG, a framework that represents this complementarity through semantic gain. Offline, a large language model assesses the relevant and nonredundant information contributed by one chunk given another within neighborhoods proposed by semantic similarity. These ordered assessments are aggregated into symmetric edge weights to construct a reusable Semantic Gain Graph. Online, controlled Personalized PageRank starts from query-relevant seeds and ranks complementary evidence beyond the initial retrieval set. Experiments on MuSiQue, 2Wiki, and HotpotQA with two language model backbones show that SG$^2$RAG achieves the best average EM, F1, and Recall@10 among the evaluated single-retrieval methods. Under identical candidate support and propagation, semantic gain improves average F1 and Recall@10 over similarity weighting by 4.08 and 4.31 points. Analyses of supporting documents and improvements in three iterative retrieval frameworks further support its effectiveness as a reusable retrieval component.

### [Gap Detection: Mitigating Homogenization through Demand and Defensibility](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20162)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.20162

> This paper on gap detection directly addresses generating diverse content, relevant to reducing collision in generated answers.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Language models often produce semantically similar answers to open-ended questions, even when many plausible alternatives exist. Generating useful alternatives requires identifying both what the query calls for and what existing responses already cover. We introduce Gap Detection, a framework that searches for relevant but unoccupied directions in a shared text embedding space. Its objective, GapScore, combines query relevance (Demand) with dissimilarity to the nearest existing item (Defensibility). The framework optimizes this product on the unit sphere, maps candidate directions to concept words with a language model, and uses task-specific filtering and constrained prompting to turn those concepts into text. On five curated topics and 90 INFINITY-CHAT queries, the reported mean relative reductions in output Collision are 12.8% and 10.8%, respectively. We also instantiate the framework for generative engine optimization over four product markets and for iterative synthetic-data augmentation. In the latter setting, with 100 training examples per method, Gap Detection reduces Collision from 0.324 to 0.298 relative to undirected accumulation and increases the Vendi Score from 27.16 to 29.20; the accuracy difference is one example on a 40-example test set. Component ablations expose the trade-off between semantic separation and relevance, while also showing that better direction scores do not uniformly yield better downstream metrics. These results support explicit gap search as a practical mechanism for diversifying content, with task validity checked separately from geometric novelty.

### [Gray-Box Hallucination Detection in LLMs with Token Identities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19996)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.19996

> Directly addresses hallucination detection in LLMs, crucial for grounded QA and safety in applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Large language models often produce fluent but factually incorrect output. Gray-box detectors identify such hallucinations from the token-probability statistics emitted during generation, avoiding the need for white-box access to hidden states that frontier models do not provide. These detectors, however, typically rely only on probability-derived representations and ignore the identities of the candidate tokens. They can therefore observe how uncertain the model is, but not what it is uncertain about. Consequently, they cannot distinguish between a model that is torn between "big" and "small" and one that is torn between "big" and "huge" when the two pairs receive the same probability distribution. We introduce GURI (Gray-box Uncertainty Representations with Identity), a lightweight gray-box hallucination detector that combines candidate-token probabilities with per-token vector identifiers. This exposes which alternatives the model considered while preserving the accessibility and transferability of the gray-box setting. Across three open-weight models and three question-answering benchmarks, GURI increases mean test ROC-AUC from 72.09% to 79.09% over a probability-only baseline. On the closed-source GPT-4.1 model, mean ROC-AUC increases from 67.39% to 71.84%. Among the three open-weight models, GURI outperforms transferred probability-only baseline in 17 of 18 directed cross-model transfers without fine-tuning. For the three open-weight models, cross-dataset transfer between HotpotQA and TriviaQA with the model held fixed improves mean ROC-AUC over transferred LOS-Net by 3.76 points.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

However, increasing context length exacerbates the trade-off between reasoning performance and inference efficiency. Inspired by Kahneman's account of fast and slow thinking, we propose MemDuet, a dual-process memory framework that learns to coordinate a small language model for fast, parallel evidence selection from raw context chunks (System 1) with a large language model for memory-update verification and memory updates in document order (System 2). ### [XViewRank: Overlap-Conditioned Score Fusion for LLM-Augmented Sparse Retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20122)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.20122

> Directly addresses retrieval strategies for enhancing sparse retrieval in LLMs, relevant for RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Large language models can reduce vocabulary mismatch in sparse retrieval by generating alternative views of a query. Once these views are searched independently, however, their ranked lists may be complementary, redundant, or affected by query drift, making fixed fusion suboptimal. We present XViewRank, a lightweight retrieval-side method for conditionally fusing BM25 results from an original query, a hypothesis view, and a step-back view. For each pooled candidate, XViewRank retains three normalized BM25 scores and three query-level overlaps between the retrieved result sets. A small nonlinear scorer uses the overlaps as context that changes how the view-specific scores are interpreted, without jointly encoding query and passage text. Under a matched-input protocol, XViewRank improves nDCG@10 and MAP numerically over reciprocal rank fusion on both TREC DL 2019 and 2020, with a statistically significant MAP gain on DL19. Additional analyses show that the clearest benefit occurs when the view rankings have low agreement, while the evidence on DL20 is weaker. The results support overlap-conditioned score fusion as a compact retrieval-side alternative to fixed fusion, while also delimiting where its advantage is empirically established.

### [Computed, not Memorized: Bit-Factorized Identifiers for Efficient Generative Retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17467)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.17467

> Proposes a novel DocID design for generative retrieval that enhances unseen document performance, relevant for RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Generative retrieval is known for its efficient end-to-end indexing and retrieval. In particular, numeric Document Identifiers (DocIDs) are generally more efficient because they are shorter and generally fixed-length sequences over a small vocabulary, requiring fewer decoding steps during beam search. However, they tend to generalize poorly to unseen documents and must be learned during indexing-stage training. In this paper, we propose a numeric DocID design for generative retrieval that enables better adaptation to unseen documents without retraining. These results show that an appropriate DocID design can heavily reduce retraining costs when adapting generative retrievers to new documents.

### [Separating Prompt Harmfulness from Interaction Safety via Partial Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16761)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.16761

> Directly relevant to safety guardrails for LLMs, addressing interaction safety during generation.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

LLM-based information systems need safety guardrails. Most guardrails classify the prompt alone. If the prompt looks harmful, the system rejects it before any answer is generated. This is costly. Many rejected prompts would have received a safe response. Users lose help they should have received from the information system. This cost grows as conversational and generative search becomes common. Prompt-level labels treat harm as a property of the query. But harm depends on what the model actually writes. Existing input-output guards do look at responses. But they judge a finished response, so the decision comes after full generation. However, we observe that safety of model interaction with the input prompt can be inferred early with only partial output. Second, we show that prompt harmfulness and interaction safety differ. Prompt labels and response labels agree on only 50.46\% of items (Cohen's $κ$ = 0.06). Of the prompts that make any model respond harmfully, only 3.5\% do so for all seven LLMs. Third, we show that partial responses carry most of the signal. Fine-tuned classifiers predict response-level harm well (best AUPRC 0.885, F1 0.807). About one third of the response tokens recover almost full-response AUPRC.

### [Recall Begets Recall: Recurrent Memory Ranking for Personalized LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16137)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.16137

> Strongly aligns with conversational memory concepts in LLMs, particularly memory ranking.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Memory ranking is the stage in personalized Large Language Models (LLMs) that decides which of the candidate memories returned by a retriever enter the limited context, so that the answer is grounded in the right parts of the user's history. Existing memory rankers operate in a single pass, scoring each candidate once against the raw query and cutting the ranked list at a fixed top-K. The single pass misses memories that the answer needs but that look unrelated to the query on their own, and the fixed cut ignores how many memories the query needs. To address both problems, we propose ReMind, a memory ranker that runs as a recurrent and self-terminating loop, following the cognitive theory of memory search: each recalled item joins the cue for the next recall (compound cueing), and recall stops once enough has been collected for the task (metamemory monitoring). For compound cueing, ReMind adds the top-ranked memory to the query context at each step and reranks the remaining candidates under the expanded cue, so that each recall makes the cue more specific and later steps look for what is still missing rather than for what merely resembles the query. For metamemory monitoring, a sufficiency predictor estimates at each step whether the collected memories are enough to answer the query and stops the loop once they are, so that the number of selected memories adapts to each query. Experiments on public benchmarks show that ReMind achieves the best results compared to state-of-the-art memory rankers.

### [Delta Tokenizer: Reference-Identical Tokenization of Session Deltas and Segment-Parallelism for Long Prompts on Multicore CPUs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19801)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.19801

> Addresses improving efficiency in tokenization, relevant for chat applications handling large inputs.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

An agent that drives an LLM sends the model the same conversation again and again, and each turn adds only a little text at the end. Inference engines reuse the KV cache of the shared prefix, so only the new tokens pass through the model. However, tokenization has no such shortcut. The CPU front end of the serving engine converts every request back into token IDs from its first character, which lengthens the time to first token (TTFT) and consumes CPU on every turn. We present Delta Tokenizer, a CPU tokenization front end that tokenizes only the delta between a request and the previous request of the same session. A shared CPU pool schedules these chunks together with the short incremental updates, so an update runs between two chunks rather than behind an entire long job. The incremental path shortens the tokenization of a session turn by roughly 40x relative to the Hugging Face reference tokenizer, and on 16 cores the chunk-parallel path encodes a four-million-character request 8.2x faster than a serial encode and 1.6-2.1x faster than LoPT. Inside SGLang, Delta Tokenizer reduces the median TTFT through the chat interface by 59% at 100K tokens and brings the P99 TTFT of agent sessions down 9.5x under load and 23x when long requests tie up the front end. Every output we checked matches the reference, among them 41.8 billion documents encoded by its fast encoder.
