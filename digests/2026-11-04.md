### [EvoMemState: Evolving Memory State for Personal Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03743)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.03743

> This work directly addresses long-term memory in chat assistants, which is crucial for building conversational memory.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Long-term memory enables large language model (LLM) agents to provide continuous personalized assistance by retaining user information across interactions. Existing systems support this process through fact extraction, organization, updating, and retrieval, but largely maintain memories as independent facts. This fact-centric paradigm may overlook how new facts update, constrain, or conflict with earlier ones, while leaving their applicability, role, and priority for the current request implicit. In this paper, we introduce EvoMemState, a state-centric framework that augments persistent facts with two complementary states: Relational States that capture how facts remain valid and relate to one another as memory evolves, and a Reasoning State that determines how these facts should be applied to the current request. Specifically, EvoMemState consists of three key components: (i) Ontology-Guided Memory State Induction extracts Persistent Facts and constructs Relational States that maintain their validity, scope, and evolving relations. (ii) RL-Based State Evolution internalizes memory-use experience into a policy through reinforcement learning to generate a request-specific Reasoning State. (iii) State-Aware Reasoning then combines the relevant facts with their Relational States and the generated Reasoning State to guide the personal agent. Extensive experiments on long-term dialogue and memory-use benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvements over existing memory systems in retrieval completeness and memory-grounded response accuracy. Our code and implementation details are available at https://github.com/XMUDeepLIT/EvoMemState.git.

### [HERIT: Enabling Global Access to Hanja-to-English Historical Translation by Mitigating Temporal Bias](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04949)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.04949

> Proposes a RAG-based approach for historical translation, directly applicable to document ingestion.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Historical archives are invaluable for multidisciplinary research but remain inaccessible globally due to severe language barriers. While manual translation is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming, simply applying existing machine translation models is inadequate due to unique linguistic nuances and the temporal bias inherent in scarce human-labeled corpora. To address these challenges, we propose HERIT, a systematic domain-adaptation pipeline that leverages retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to generate high-quality pseudo-labeled data from abundant Hanja-Korean corpora. This approach substantially expands the training dataset, mitigating both data scarcity and temporal overfitting across different historical periods. Extensive automated and human evaluations demonstrate that HERIT consistently outperforms baselines. Finally, we translate previously untranslated portions of the archives using our model, thereby enabling global access to these resources for researchers.

### [When Ground Truth Is Not Ground Truth: Automatic Labeling for Hallucination Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04697)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.04697

> Focuses on hallucination detection relevant to evaluation of RAG systems.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Hallucinations in Large Language Models (LLMs) remain a major challenge, as they can produce misleading, factually incorrect, or unverifiable information, thereby undermining their trustworthiness and reliability. However, reliable ground-truth labels remain a key challenge in evaluating hallucination detectors. Recent studies often construct labels automatically by comparing model generations with reference answers using similarity metrics such as BLEURT. This implicitly treats BLEURT similarity as a proxy for hallucination, despite its English-centric supervision, unbounded score range, and design as a similarity metric rather than a factuality measure, making threshold-based ground-truth construction potentially unreliable. Through comprehensive experiments using 15 automatic supervision judges, seven hallucination detection baselines, two generator models, two QA datasets, and five languages, we demonstrate that detector performance varies substantially with the choice of supervision judge. Human validation reveals substantial differences in labeling reliability, with QGen-9B achieving the lowest overall misclassification rate (12.86%), while BLEURT and BLEURT-n exhibit rates of 47.23% and 47.32%, respectively. Our findings highlight the limitations of automatic similarity-based labeling and the need for more reliable judging methods that better capture semantic and factual correctness.

### [Voluntary Servitude: Weaponized Safety Warning Mechanisms for Controlling AI Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04393)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.04393

> Discusses security concerns regarding LLM agents which is vital for safety in RAG applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

LLM agents increasingly act on information retrieved from public sources they treat as credible, which turns a manipulated recommendation into a transaction, a dependency, or a decision with physical consequences. The security literature models this threat as instruction injection, in which an adversary writes a command into retrieved content and deployed defenses learn to recognize command syntax. We show that an attacker can instead steer an agent through the agent's own harm-avoidance. A single fabricated hazard, written as an ordinary review, leads the agent to disqualify the legitimate options and select the attacker's target by its own reasoning, with no command for a detector to recognize. We call this voluntary servitude. These payloads steer agents about as often as instruction injection (60.4% vs. 66.0%), and deployed injection detectors score them as benign, so behind a filter they are 1.7-4.4x more effective. We measure this at the generation stage across six models and ten consumer-service domains, for three attack families, and show that it transfers to production browser agents, outperforming instruction injection, and to a retrieval pipeline (1.0-57.0% end-to-end success, depending on the retriever). We then show that a corroboration requirement added to the agent's instructions removes roughly 95% of the exposure against a non-adaptive attacker without retraining. With the requirement in place, the agent still reads the planted hazard and no longer acts on it.

</details>

### [WASP-Mem: Let Agent Organize Procedural Memory. Human Spend Effort on Diagnosis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04003)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.04003

> Introduces procedural memory for agents, very relevant for context management in chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

LLM agents struggle to automate complex enterprise procedures because static, pre-built harness cannot capture proprietary policies, undocumented behaviors, or state-dependent recovery paths that surface only during execution. Procedural memory accrued from agent experience offers a training-free path to closing this gap -- yet unlike episodic records or semantic facts, it must consolidate repeated successes and diagnosed failures into reusable actions and recovery strategies. We introduce WASP-Mem (Wiki-based Agent-Synthesized Procedural Memory), a nimble but effective method that distills execution traces and feedback into persistent, interlinked wiki pages, enabling continual agent learning. On TheAgentCompany benchmark, WASP-Mem raises the checkpoint pass rate by 4.6-6.1% over the strongest baseline we compared across three models while requiring 57-61% less storage. We further present a systematic controlled study spanning consolidation method, feedback type, trace ingestion scope, and refinement rounds. Our results reveal that prescriptive knowledge organization such as human defined schemas and explicit deduplication rules hurts performance rather than closing the procedural-knowledge gap. In contrast, human diagnosis converts failed traces into corrective knowledge and drives sustained improvement: a single round of human diagnosis outperforms four rounds of self-judgment by 7.0-10.9%. These findings point to a simple division of labor: let agents organize procedural memory, and invest human effort where it matters most -- diagnosis.

</details>

### [Scaling Laws For Data-Constrained Dense Retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04979)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.04979

> Focuses on scaling laws for dense retrieval models, directly relevant to retrieval performance and optimization in RAG systems.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Dense retrieval models are typically obtained by fine-tuning pretrained language models on supervised retrieval data, where high-quality annotations or interaction logs are often limited and reused across many epochs. This data-constrained regime differs from the abundant-data setting assumed by standard scaling laws, making it unclear how the best attainable retrieval performance changes with model size and unique fine-tuning data. We study this question through the minimum achievable evaluation loss (MAEL), defined as the lowest evaluation loss reached during training under fixed model-size and data-budget constraints. We first show that learning-rate choices strongly affect optimization trajectories but have little effect on the final attainable MAEL: after rescaling the optimization-step axis, learning curves collapse across learning rates, and CKA analysis shows that checkpoints reaching MAEL learn highly similar representations. We then fine-tune 35 BERT-family dense retrievers on MS MARCO subsets and find that MAEL is accurately described by a simple multiplicative power law over non-embedding parameters and unique samples, outperforming commonly used additive scaling formulations. We further derive a scaling law for the training step at which MAEL is reached and show that optimal weight decay follows an approximately invariant EMA timescale, yielding lower MAEL and stronger retrieval performance on both in-domain and out-of-domain benchmarks. Finally, combining loss and step scaling suggests that, in our observed regime, increasing pretrained model size is approximately 1.46 times as fine-tuning-compute-efficient as increasing unique in-domain data for reducing MAEL.

### [QueryFlow: Multi-Hop Graph Retrieval with Adaptive Query Evolution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04625)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.04625

> Directly addresses multi-hop retrieval in the context of retrieval-augmented generation, highly relevant.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multi-hop retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) supports Web information seeking and fact verification by recovering complementary evidence across related pages within a limited retrieval budget. Scoring evidence independently against the original question can miss supporting units whose relevance emerges through intermediate facts. Iterative retrieval incorporates these facts into subsequent queries, while graph retrieval explicitly models relations among evidence units. Yet fixed-query graph retrievers update entity representations without adapting the query condition to accumulated relational context. We view multi-hop evidence recovery as the coupled evolution of evidence representations and query conditions, with accumulated context informing both what the retriever represents and how it searches for complementary evidence. QueryFlow realizes this perspective by recurrently integrating query-conditioned summaries of graph context into a shared query state, which controls boundary inputs and relation features for subsequent propagation. Across four representative multi-hop question answering (QA) datasets, QueryFlow achieves macro Recall@10 (R@10), Full Recall@10 (FR@10), and LLM-Acc gains of 4.98, 8.40, and 8.0 percentage points over representative baselines, respectively.

### [ReuseGR: Offline--Online Decoupled Generative Retrieval via Adaptive Prefix Reuse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04259)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.04259

> Addresses generative retrieval, which is crucial for optimizing retrieval approaches in RAG architectures.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Larger generative retrieval models can improve retrieval quality, but their decoding cost makes low-latency serving difficult. When different users submit the same query, generating complete Semantic IDs (SIDs) for every request repeats computation, while their interaction histories call for different results. The challenge is to reuse large-model computation across requests while keeping retrieval personalized. We propose \textbf{ReuseGR}, which separates query-level candidate planning from request-level personalized completion. A 7B offline planner generates SID prefix sets shared across requests with the same query. A 0.5B online model uses each request's history to select prefixes and generate the remaining tokens. ReuseCut chooses a cache depth for each query based on historical coverage, balancing computation reuse and candidate coverage. To strengthen online completion, we transfer 7B suffix-scoring knowledge to 0.5B through path-level knowledge distillation (Path KD). BudgetCut then selects scored branches to improve final top-100 recall under a fixed branch budget. Its selection policy is initialized by on-policy distillation (OPD) and refined through group-relative policy optimization (GRPO). On an industrial search-advertising dataset with approximately 2M query--item records and 3.38M catalog items, ReuseGR improves query-level HR@100 by 3.83 percentage points and reduces mean online latency by 38.9\% relative to an all-online 0.5B baseline when prefixes are prepared. It achieves HR@100 comparable to an all-online 7B baseline with 57.7\% lower online latency. A 14-day user-level online A/B test against the 0.5B baseline shows relative gains of 0.93\% in click-through rate and 1.05\% in revenue per thousand requests.

</details>

### [Modular and Efficient Approximate Nearest Neighbors Search with Vectorium and kANNolo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03927)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.03927

> This paper is highly relevant due to its focus on efficient approximate nearest neighbors search, which is essential for vector search in RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Approximate Nearest Neighbors (ANN) search over neural embeddings powers modern retrieval systems such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Embeddings come in three representations (dense, sparse and multi-vector), and efficient search combines an index structure, a similarity function and a quantization scheme. Existing libraries fix one combination, hard-code each one separately, or factor only one side of the problem, and none covers all three representations. We present Vectorium and kANNolo, two open-source Rust libraries that split this design space into a data-management layer and an indexing layer. Vectorium abstracts the vector, the distance function, the quantizer and the dataset independently, and provides 11 encoders, from 32-bit floats to sub-bit codes. On top of it, kANNolo implements Hierarchical Navigable Small-World (HNSW) graph and Inverted Vector File (IVF) indexes that are generic over representation, encoder and metric, and a two-stage index that composes any first-stage index with any rescoring dataset. Since every combination is specialized at compile time, this modularity adds no run-time overhead, and the resulting indexes reach state-of-the-art performance: our Product Quantization matches Faiss in accuracy at competitive speed, our RaBitQ and HNSW match or outperform their reference implementations, and our IVF outperforms Faiss. Against six dense and five sparse systems on seven public collections, our indexes give the best memory-throughput trade-off on dense data in 9 of 12 collection-accuracy pairs and perform well on out-of-distribution queries. On sparse data, the systems built on Vectorium lead on all three collections, with Seismic and SeismicWave the fastest and kANNolo's indexes the most compact.

### [VIGOR: Visual Gain Ordering for Hallucination Mitigation in Multimodal Discrete Diffusion Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05066)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CV` · 2611.05066

> Addresses hallucination mitigation in multimodal models, relevant to grounded generation concerns.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multimodal discrete diffusion language models (dLLMs) generate responses through iterative mask prediction and offer a promising alternative to autoregressive large vision-language models. However, they still suffer from hallucination, producing outputs that are plausible under language priors but insufficiently grounded in the image. We argue that hallucination in multimodal dLLMs is not only a token prediction problem, but also a premature commitment problem: during iterative denoising, some masked positions are unmasked too early because their confidence is driven more by language priors than by visual evidence, and these early commitments can bias later generation. To address this issue, we propose Visual Gain Ordering (VIGOR), a training-free and plug-and-play inference strategy that reorders masked positions according to counterfactual visual evidence. At each denoising step, VIGOR compares the confidence of each candidate token under the original image-conditioned pass and an additional visual-ablation pass, and prioritizes positions whose predictions are more strongly supported by the image. Experiments on LLaDA-V and Lumina-DiMOO show that VIGOR consistently reduces hallucination and improves multimodal reasoning performance across benchmarks. Code is available at https://github.com/THU-BPM/Vigor.

</details>

### [OCR in the Foundation Model Era: From Specialist Models to Unified Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04874)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CV` · 2611.04874

> Examines OCR's evolution and issues in document representation, directly relevant to document ingestion for RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is among the oldest and most widely deployed applications of artificial intelligence. This survey reviews its development, focusing on the Foundation Model Era. We define OCR through faithful transcription: within a specified visual scope, OCR should completely reconstruct the text, notation, and document structure observable in the source image, and every textual, notational, or structural element in the output must be verifiable from visual evidence in that image. We trace the development from specialist OCR models toward unified models, examining joint text detection and recognition, structured sequence generation, and the use of pretrained vision-language models (VLMs). We organize contemporary OCR systems into five coexisting families: modular toolkits and task-specific models, specialized document VLMs, hybrid workflows, general-purpose VLMs, and commercial products and managed services. We compare their architectures, data, and training, examining hallucination and omission, insufficient spatial grounding, and inference cost in generative OCR. We review evaluation from character and scene text recognition to parsing individual structural elements and full-page transcription, highlighting gaps in consistent output definitions, attribution of performance to individual components, and coverage of real capture conditions and visible content and structure. Finally, we summarize the challenges facing OCR and discuss directions for future research. We continuously track related work at https://github.com/zhihengli-casia/Awesome-OCR-in-the-Foundation-Model-Era.

### [Knowingly Wrong: Training LLMs Against Their Own Beliefs can Induce Misalignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05287)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.05287

> Relevant to fine-tuning risks and potential misalignment in LLMs used in RAG features.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Fine-tuning a large language model (LLM) on harmful data in a narrow domain, such as bad medical advice, can make it broadly misaligned. How realistic this risk is remains unclear, since models are very rarely fine-tuned on explicitly harmful data in practice. In this work, we show that fine-tuning on data the model believes to be false but that has no harmful content, can also induce broad misalignment. Across mathematics, trivia, cooking and sports, the more strongly the model disbelieves the answers it is trained on, the more misaligned it becomes. In mathematics, misaligned answers rise from 4% to over 60% as the training answers become more wrong, with little loss of general capability. After fine-tuning, the model states the wrong answers but its multiple-choice probabilities still prefer the true ones, which suggests that misalignment comes from this internal inconsistency. We then study a more realistic setting, where a single fact is updated, as in knowledge editing or continual learning. The same relationship holds and the more the model still believes the true fact after training on the false one, the more misaligned it becomes. When we frame the same false fact as part of a coherent story, the model comes to believe it more deeply and becomes less misaligned than when we instruct it to assert the false fact. Content filters and persona vectors cannot tell these two framings apart, even though they lead to different levels of misalignment. These results suggest a risk for model updates, where training a model that does not adopt the new fact as true can induce misalignment even when the data looks harmless.

### [SLATE: A Large-Scale Benchmark for Long-Horizon API Selection in E-commerce Tool Use](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03982)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.03982

> Presents a benchmark for API selection, relevant for tool use in enterprise systems with RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Enterprises increasingly want LLM agents to automate standard operating procedures over their software stacks -- mapping each step of a procedure to the right call among thousands of internal APIs, without hand-authored step-to-API mappings. The core difficulty is API selection at scale: choosing and composing the correct tools over a long horizon, judged only by whether the overall task succeeds. Current tool-use benchmarks do not test this regime -- they expose only a handful of tools, evaluate single-step invocation, or grade multi-step behavior with subjective LLM-as-a-judge scores that penalize valid alternative trajectories. We introduce SLATE (Synthetic Large-scale API Toolkit for E-commerce), a large-scale benchmark that tests large-toolset, long-horizon API selection in a realistic enterprise domain. SLATE couples (i) queries decomposed into hierarchical, tool-grounded procedures over a ~1,000-tool e-commerce library, (ii) a deterministic, context-aware simulator that returns recorded outputs for matching calls and default responses otherwise, and (iii) an end-to-end task-success metric -- a task succeeds when it reaches every reference outcome -- that credits diverse-but-valid trajectories while remaining fully automatic and reproducible. Experiments with six open decision models and the language models they are built on, from 3B to 12B parameters, show that even the best model completes only 5.0% of the tasks, establishing SLATE as a challenging testbed for reliable enterprise tool use. We release SLATE, its simulator, and the evaluation harness at https://geshijoker.github.io/slate/

### [EvoGroup: A Self-Evolving Harness for Group Agents in Shared Contexts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03868)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.03868

> The paper discusses conversational memory and context management for group agents, relevant for long-term memory in chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

With the rise of Group Agent such as Claude Tag, agents increasingly serve as digital workers for long-term team collaboration. These agents operate over shared contexts that continuously accumulate through multi-user interactions. However, existing agent memory systems are mainly designed for individual users and struggle to capture group-level knowledge in shared contexts, including group relationships and collaboratively formed knowledge. Manual memory strategies further limit adaptation to diverse and evolving shared contexts. To this end, we propose \textbf{EvoGroup (EvoG)}, a self-evolving harness for group agents that starts from a minimal six-tool cold start and adapts through interaction with shared context. EvoG uses confidence and task outcomes to select informative trajectories, while also eliciting self-reflections on uncertainty in low-confidence cases. The analysis agent then examines selected trajectories through adaptive progressive disclosure, followed by bucketed analysis to synthesize high-level findings that guide the evolve agent in revising the Harness. Empirically, pass@1 on held-out split of EverMemBench improves from 71.07\% to 89.58\%, surpassing both self-evolving memory and human-designed harnesses, including Claude Code (85.83\%). To evaluate generalization, we freeze the evolved harness and evaluate it on the unseen GroupMemBench, where, with GPT-5.5, it demonstrates effective generalization and outperforms both memory baselines and human-designed harnesses.

### [Learning Where to Look: Observation Policy Optimization for Thinking with Images](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04943)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CV` · 2611.04943

> Involves optimizing observational actions in visual question answering, which is relevant to grounded generation.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Complex visual question answering often requires vision-language models to think with images, i.e., actively acquire local visual evidence by zooming into informative regions of high-resolution images. Our error analysis shows that many failures in this setting originate from incorrect observation actions, where the model fails to localize key regions or obtain crops that contain sufficient evidence for answering. We further observe that bounding-box tokens exhibit substantially higher generation entropy than ordinary language tokens, indicating high uncertainty when the model decides where to look. However, existing reinforcement learning methods based on final-answer rewards provide only trajectory-level feedback, making it difficult to assign precise credit to the observation actions. We propose Observation Policy Optimization (OPO), an RL algorithm for optimizing observation actions in tool-augmented thinking with images. OPO consists of two core modules. First, Uncertainty-Guided Observation Branching (UOB) treats each image zoom-in tool call as an observation event and selectively branches at high-uncertainty, low-redundancy events during rollout, enabling efficient exploration of alternative cropping decisions and their resulting visual evidence. Second, Evidence-Localized Advantage Attribution (ELAA) compares sibling branches using local evidence-sufficiency rewards and assigns credit only to the corresponding observation-action tokens. By decoupling observation-action optimization from trajectory-level outcome supervision, OPO improves visual evidence acquisition and further enhances visual reasoning performance. Experiments show that OPO consistently improves Qwen3-VL models across scales and achieves competitive performance against larger models and tool-augmented baselines. Code is publicly available.

</details>
