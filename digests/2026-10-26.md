### [ActionRAG: Evidence-Refining Graph Diffusion for Long-Term Memory in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32752)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.32752

> Introduces a novel memory architecture for LLMs, highly relevant to conversational memory for chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

LLM agents need long-term memory that can recover precise and mutually consistent evidence from growing interaction histories, particularly when answers depend on relations among facts rather than isolated semantic matches. Current memory pipelines remain vulnerable at two stages: indexing errors can fragment repeated entities or merge distinct ones, and online retrieval often treats initial similarity scores as final even when the surrounding graph provides evidence for revising them. We introduce ActionRAG, a graph memory architecture that couples cautious indexing with query-time graph retroevaluation. Its indexing pipeline reconciles type labels and applies tiered entity resolution. Given a query, dual-channel entity and triple retrieval forms an initial evidence set and induces a bounded local graph. Query-conditioned receiver-normalized diffusion refines triple scores using local path structure. Seed protection, hub-penalized flux, and triple materialization preserve direct evidence while recovering missed connections. We evaluate ActionRAG on MemoryAgentBench (MAB), which tests accurate retrieval, conflict resolution, long-range understanding, and test-time learning. Across its eight deterministic-scoring columns, ActionRAG achieves 55.1 average accuracy, improving over the strongest retrieval-based memory baseline by 5.4 points. Under MAB's query-time cost accounting, mean synthesis cost stays below \$0.001 per query on the four reported cost columns, and the sum of separately measured retrieval-side p99 components is 440.7 ms on our consumer Apple Silicon setup.

</details>

### [From Noisy Retrieval to Decision Evidence: Silver Evidence Demonstrations for In-Context Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32128)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.32128

> Directly addresses improving retrieval quality for decision-making in RAG contexts.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

RAG and retrieval-augmented few-shot ICL can struggle when decision-relevant evidence is buried in long, noisy context. Retrieved passages and demonstrations may contain redundant or distracting information. Although expert-labeled development examples may identify the text supporting each decision, these annotations are typically used only as answer-side rationales rather than as supervision for filtering noisy retrieval. We propose silver-evidence demonstrations, which replace raw retrieved passages in ICL examples with generated, target-conditioned evidence summaries. For each labeled development example, an LLM generates silver evidence without access to the expert label or gold evidence; this evidence is then paired with the expert label and gold evidence where available. At test time, a single structured call generates silver evidence from retrieved passages and then predicts the final label and decision evidence. Across EI 2.0, SciFact, synthetic policy retrieval, and HICRIC/IMR data, our full method improves over equally sized gold-only ICL (label-only for policy) on three of four datasets and improves evidence F1 on all annotated datasets. It also achieves higher mean label accuracy than InstructRAG-style ICL on all four datasets. These results suggest that replacing demonstration-side retrieved passages with compact, target-conditioned silver evidence is particularly useful for decisions based on long and noisy text, where it reduces distracting context while preserving decision-relevant signals and expert supervision.

</details>

### [PersonalQuery-Benchmark:Evaluating Retrieval Robustness to Personalized User Expression Differences](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32164)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.32164

> Introduces a benchmark for evaluating personalized expression differences, which is relevant for retrieval robustness in RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Existing personalized information retrieval methods mainly model differences in user needs through user preferences and query intent, while paying little attention to the personalized expression differences that arise when different users express the same retrieval need. Existing real query data typically lack stable user identities, long-term expression histories, and intent-matched counterparts, making it difficult to systematically evaluate the impact of personalized expression differences on retrieval outcomes. To this end, we propose PersonalQuery-Benchmark (PQB). PQB models personalized syntactic expression and personalized writing errors from users' historical texts, constructs personalized syntactic expression queries and personalized writing-error queries, and validates benchmark quality through LLM assessment and independent human evaluation. PQB covers three Amazon product domains with 6,516 query instances in total, and is evaluated on five retrieval models representing lexical, learned sparse, dense bi-encoder, late-interaction, and hybrid architectures, together with two LLM-based ranking models. Results show that even when the information need is held fixed, personalized expression differences still induce measurable ranking variation; different retrieval models exhibit clearly different sensitivities to syntactic variation and writing-error variation, while higher overall retrieval effectiveness does not necessarily correspond to stronger robustness against expression variation. PQB provides a new benchmark and evaluation framework for systematically studying personalized expression robustness. The associated code and dataset are available at the following address: https://github.com/cpxjazz123/PersonalQuery-Benchmark.

</details>

### [MICE to Meet You: From Re-ranking Only to End-to-End Retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31932)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.31932

> The paper addresses retrieval pipelines with an emphasis on efficiency and effectiveness, crucial for RAG architectures.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Late-interaction models offer state-of-the-art efficiency-effectiveness trade-offs for neural retrieval by enabling document representations to be pre-computed and efficiently indexed. Yet, their relatively simple interaction mechanisms, typically MaxSim, can limit their effectiveness compared to cross-encoders. MICE, a recently introduced re-ranking architecture, aims to combine cross-encoder-like effectiveness through expressive transformer-based scoring with reduced inference-time computation through document-side pre-computation. However, no practical way to store these document representations was explored. We address this limitation here and introduce MICE-P, the first end-to-end retrieval pipeline with MICE, based on PLAID to index the contextualized document token representations. We conduct a controlled comparison of MICE, ColBERT and cross-encoders by training comparable models using the Ettin Family under matched experimental conditions. Our results show that MICE-P defines a new class of retrieval architecture with an efficiency-effectiveness trade-off of its own: it matches late-interaction retrieval throughput while retaining the effectiveness of cross-encoders.

</details>

### [System One models Are Not Better Rerankers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32318)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.32318

> Focuses on reranking, which is relevant for improving retrieval quality in RAG systems.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

System One models, or decision models, answer a question about an input with a calibrated probability instead of generated text. Within weeks of the first release, in September 2026, they were used as rerankers. We test whether the decision fine-tuning behind them makes a better reranker than plain reranker fine-tuning of the same model. For six Qwen backbones from 0.6B to 27B parameters and ten TREC DL and BEIR collections, we compare the untuned model, asked zero-shot, with its decision fine-tunes and its reranker fine-tunes, two of which we train ourselves. In our work, we draw the conclusion that decision fine-tuning never makes the better reranker. From 0.6B to 9B, reranker fine-tuning wins on every backbone by 5 to 29 points nDCG@10, also on zero-shot collections, and at 27B the two tie. Up to 9B, decision fine-tuning adds nothing over simply asking the untuned model. Moreover, we find that the calibration advantage of decision models depends on the question. Asked whether a passage answers the query, they are better calibrated than the published reranker fine-tunes from 4B on. Asked a slightly different question from prior work, they lose this advantage. Lastly, calibration does not make up for a worse ranking: up to 9B, a reranker fine-tune keeps the better passages at a fixed threshold. If you need a reranker, fine-tune a reranker.

</details>

### [Head Dimension Matters in Language Models for Long-Context Retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32213)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.32213

> Discusses how head dimensions affect long-context retrieval performance, directly relevant to RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multi-head attention retrieves and combines context representations through query--key interactions and value aggregation, with head dimension constraining the per-head representation space. In long-context processing, it remains unclear whether allocating a fixed parameter budget to fewer, wider heads improves information retrieval. In this paper, we investigate how head dimension affects the performance of long-context retrieval tasks. Our empirical studies reveal that pretraining loss and short-context non-retrieval task performance are not affected much by head dimension, while long-context retrieval performance improves with wider heads at fixed model sizes. We find that models with wider heads have a higher proportion of retrieval heads, associating more parameters with retrieval. Our ablation studies also show that the retrieval performance improves with wider heads even when the projection weights in attention modules are frozen during long-context continual pretraining, suggesting that wider heads not only benefit updates in attention modules but lead to more effective adaptation of components such as embeddings and MLPs.

</details>

### [SOPO: Socratic Guided Policy Optimization for Span-Level Hallucination Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32212)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.32212

> The paper focuses on hallucination detection in LLMs, relevant to ensuring answer faithfulness.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Large Language Models (LLMs) have gained widespread adoption in various natural language processing tasks, but suffer from hallucination issues where they generate unfaithful or inconsistent content. While Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has shown promising applications in reasoning tasks, its application to span-level hallucination detection is challenged by localization ambiguity, reward sparsity on hard examples, and uncontrolled length inflation. To address these issues, we introduce Socratic Guided Policy Optimization (SOPO), a multi-turn RL framework for robust hallucination detection. The key innovation of SOPO is a Socratic feedback mechanism, where a teacher model guides the reasoning trajectories during rollout by providing indirect hints inferred from the ground truth. This teacher-guided approach enables the model to generate more effective reasoning trajectories for hard examples, thus alleviating reward sparsity. Furthermore, we incorporate Turn-based Advantage Scaling to penalize verbosity bias, encouraging the model to develop efficient and autonomous reasoning capabilities. Empirical results on RAGTruth demonstrate that SOPO (4B) achieves state-of-the-art performance, surpassing leading proprietary models (e.g., GPT-5) and standard RL baselines, while obtaining higher reported mean scores than GRPO on four out-of-domain benchmarks, with mixed evidence against CAPO. We release our training and evaluation code alongside the 1.7B and 4B SOPO models at https://github.com/patsnap/SOPO.

</details>

### [Context or Knowledge: How LLMs Attribute Their Own Answers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32109)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.32109

> Explores source attribution, which may impact retrieval and grounded generation in RAG applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Language models can answer a question from a supplied document or from their parametric knowledge. When later asked where the answer came from, what determines their source report? We study this by intervening on the model's key-value (KV) cache after the answer is generated and before the source question is asked. On a controlled factual task with four models (7B-32B), changing or removing the document's cache never changes the report. In contrast, transplanting the question-and-answer (Q+A) cache between runs with identical question and answer tokens transfers it: every eligible report follows the donor, while the recipient's document is unchanged. On the two smaller models, steering Q+A values along directions fitted before any source question reverses 98.9-100% of reports across four document conditions at a fixed strength, while norm-matched random directions flip at most 1.1%. The components of keys and values along fitted directions carry the transplant effect: copying them alone nearly reproduces the report change; holding them fixed nearly prevents it. Applied during answer generation, the same value directions also change which source supplies the answer, though more weakly and depending on the prompt. Source reporting in this setting thus depends causally on cached state produced while answering. We do not establish general reporting accuracy or a complete interpretation of this state.

</details>

### [Agentic Memory Management Is the Next Frontier of Information Retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32464)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.32464

> It addresses memory management for AI agents, which is crucial for conversational memory in chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Information retrieval has long been focusing on connecting people with information they need to complete their tasks. Recently, the meaning of this has changed dramatically as more and more AI agents now consume, retain, and act on information on a user's behalf. Consequently, information management now also takes place \emph{inside} the agent in the form of memory management. In this perspective paper, we argue that \emph{agentic memory management} (AMM), which studies the structure, analysis, organization, storage, searching, retrieval, and \emph{use} of information in an intelligent agent's memory, is a natural extension of IR research and an area in which the IR community can make distinctive contributions. Specifically, we first present a system-level framework for AMM and introduce a computational taxonomy of memory objects as \emph{constants}, \emph{functions}, and \emph{operators}, each with distinct retrieval semantics. Then, we compare AMM with established IR research and identify their difference and connections. Last but not least, we outline a research agenda covering benchmarks, candidate principles of memory utility, optimization procedures that mix parametric and non-parametric updates, and more. We hope this paper could attract more people, both inside and outside the IR communities, to think more about AMM problems and research from IR perspectives.

</details>

### [The Answer Knows Where to Look: Zooming before MLLMs Answer under Visual-Token Budgets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32664)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CV` · 2610.32664

> Explores attention allocation for MLLMs, relevant to grounded answer retrieval in chat.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) read images through visual tokens, and under the small per-image budgets that efficient deployment requires, the fine text of documents, charts and infographics becomes illegible. Zooming in recovers the evidence, but the region must be chosen before the model answers. We find that MLLMs know where the evidence is, but not in a form that can be read before they answer: the answer tokens localize it from the middle layers on, whereas the attention available before answering does so only in the top layers, at a depth that varies across models, and diffusely. Supervising the pre-answer attention to point degrades reading, and the benefit of zooming is governed by each image's information gap. Building on these findings, we introduce ours, which lets an MLLM look where its answer would. A dedicated look token, processed in a single prefill and kept out of the answering pass, learns in hindsight to attend as the model's own correct answers do, extracting this knowledge into a concentrated pointer before any answer exists. Its hidden state then selects how to look, from no zoom to crops, square windows, windows that adapt to the pointer and foveated warps, supervised by the counterfactual outcome of every view. ours needs no box annotations, per-model layer search, external localizers or generation before the answer. On Qwen3-VL at three scales and five text-rich benchmarks, it surpasses the strongest baseline by 4.8-9.1 points at 98 visual tokens per view and attention-guided zooming by 11.1-17.0 points, while running faster than attention-guided and grounding-based zooming.

</details>
