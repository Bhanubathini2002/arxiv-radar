### [Weaver: Weaving Preference Evidence through Incremental Reasoning for Personalized Retrieval-Augmented Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05900)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.05900

> Directly discusses personalized retrieval-augmented generation, crucial for building RAG chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Personalized retrieval-augmented generation requires extracting and applying user preferences from extended interaction histories. This task is challenging because preference evidence is scattered across past conversations and remains bound to specific temporal and situational contexts. Consequently, isolated fragments rarely provide complete support for a response, while preferences expressed under different conditions are easily conflated. To address these limitations, we propose Weaver, a framework that incrementally weaves memory-conditioned retrieval trajectories with compositional preference reasoning for personalized responses. Weaver operates on a Dual-Layer Retrieval and Reasoning Graph that couples an upper Intent Dependency Graph with a lower Memory-Conditioned Retrieval Trajectory Graph. Dependency Information Gap Tracking represents the information needed to answer the current request and its prerequisite dependencies in the upper graph. Memory-Conditioned Incremental Retrieval uses established premises and clues from retrieved conversations to locate missing evidence, recording the resulting retrieval trajectories in the lower graph. Scope-Aware Compositional Reasoning jointly interprets the accumulated evidence to determine which preferences apply to the request. It identifies complementary and alternative support and distinguishes conflicting claims within the same temporal and situational context. These conclusions iteratively update task premises and remaining information gaps, guiding subsequent retrieval to establish a coherent contextual basis for the final response. Experiments on personalized RAG benchmarks, particularly PersonaMem, demonstrate that Weaver outperforms leading baselines in both evidence completeness and answer quality.

### [LibriSpeech-Real-QA: A Benchmark for Streaming Question Answering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06496)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.06496

> Directly develops a benchmark for streaming question answering, a must-read.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Most speech large language models (SLMs) still process speech in an offline manner. They only start responding after they receive the complete speech segment. However, many real-world applications require streaming speech interaction, for which the audio continuously flows in and the machine is required to answer questions based on the so far received audio chunks. For example, a user may ask the system to continuously count how many times a person's name has been mentioned while they are speaking. We observe a lack of benchmarks and evaluation protocols for real-time speech interaction. Motivated by this, we propose LibriSpeech-Real-QA, a dataset and a benchmark for real-time speech interaction. We construct different types of real-time question-answer pairs from LibriSpeech to evaluate whether speech LLMs can identify target words in streaming audio. We evaluate several popular real-time commercial models and find that they still tend to respond only after the speech turn has finished. We demonstrate that we can enable a pretrained SLM to perform this task, where we explore several modeling designs and establish a solid baseline for future research.

### [ArcRAG: Agentic Compositional Retrieval over Knowledge Graphs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06481)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.06481

> Introduces a compositional retrieval strategy for RAG; a must-read.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Graph-based retrieval-augmented generation (GraphRAG) answers questions by retrieving over a knowledge graph built from a corpus. Yet retrieval strategies are designed around the structure their own construction pipeline produces, and whether they survive a change of construction is rarely tested. We study this cross-construction portability: whether one retrieval procedure, with no construction-specific schema, relation names, or retuning, stays effective when the graph is built differently. We introduce ArcRAG, which induces a query-local graph interface at inference time (the node labels, relation descriptors, and property keys reachable from the entities a question names), plans multi-hop traversals against it, and executes and repairs them as Cypher. We build each of the MuSiQue, HotpotQA, and TriviaQA corpora as a schema-guided graph (typed nodes, a closed relation set, source provenance) and as an open-information-extraction graph (untyped nodes, free-text relations), with the retriever's configuration fixed across them. We compare against a construction-agnostic Direct-Cypher agent, ablate each stage across retry and pass budgets on both graph families, and compare with results reported for prior GraphRAG systems, most of which design retrieval together with their own graph construction. Across the two constructions, we find that retrieval need not be co-designed with the graph: query-time interface induction keeps a construction-agnostic retriever effective when the construction changes, although construction still shapes absolute accuracy.

### [RAGCompass: Generalizable Retriever Routing via Reranking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05596)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.05596

> Directly addresses reranking and generalizable routing in RAG architectures, essential for effective retrieval.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Different retrievers exist for retrieval-augmented generation, yet none performs best for all queries. Therefore, the key question is: given a query and retrieved passages from different retrievers, which one is most suited for that query? Existing routers usually make predictions from query-specific features and a fixed pool of retrievers; such routers have no output for an unseen retriever and are incapable by design on a new corpus. To fill these gaps, RAGCompass reads the retrieved passage sets themselves: passages from an unfamiliar retriever or corpus stay directly comparable to other passages, so routing stays well designed where a query-only map would fail. We contrastively fine-tune a reranker against the answer each evidence set produces, which accounts for most of the gain; a small comparison head adds a moderate improvement, and neither component has retriever-specific parameters. RAGCompass improves answer F1 by 28% relative to the best fixed retriever, and outperforms fusion baselines, three query-only routers, and a prompted 8B language model. Generalization experiments show that RAGCompass keeps most of its gain under an unseen corpus, retriever, or reader, while query-only baselines fall to the best fixed retriever or below.

</details>

### [Turbocharging PiPNN for Proximity and $k$-NN Graph Building](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05690)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.05690

> Discusses optimizing proximity graphs for NN search, relevant for efficiency in vector databases.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Graph-based Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search (ANNS) indexes such as HNSW and Vamana deliver state-of-the-art query performance at the cost of large indexing times. The recently introduced PiPNN method drastically accelerated index construction by way of a partitioning-based algorithm leveraging dense matrix multiplication and top-$K$ aggregation kernels. In this paper, we unlock the full potential of PiPNN's hardware-friendly algorithm design. We introduce PiPNN II, which optimizes multiple steps of the PiPNN recipe, giving up to $8\times$ speedups for graph-based ANNS index construction. In addition, we present GPiPNN, a GPU implementation which builds comparable ANNS indices up to $43.7\times$ faster than Jasper and up to $50\times$ faster than CAGRA. We also extend PiPNN II and GPiPNN to compute high-quality $k$-NN graphs directly, which unlocks massive speedups over methods used in many existing systems for clustering, anomaly detection, and more. For 10-NN graph building at $90\%$ recall, we obtain up to a $13\times$ speedup on CPUs and $6.6\times$ on GPUs as compared to the prior best methods. PiPNN II can build a billion-scale 10-NN graph in under 16 minutes. Common to both PiPNN II and GPiPNN is the use of quantization to reduce memory traffic and leverage fast low-precision matrix operations. Top-$K$ emerges as a new bottleneck, which we optimize with specialized code for specific $K$, and by fusing with the matrix kernel to avoid materializing the distance matrix to memory. On CPUs, we demonstrate further performance improvements by leveraging our matrix kernels in the RobustPrune procedure proposed in Vamana and used in PiPNN. On GPUs, we flatten recursive partitioning into segmented batches, use one fused top-$K$ abstraction for partitioning and leaf scoring, and prune with already-witnessed direct edges rather than new candidate-to-candidate scoring passes.

</details>

### [DreamRealm: Exploring and Materializing Hallucination Worlds for Trustworthy Vision-Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06433)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CV` · 2611.06433

> Directly addresses hallucination issues in multimodal models, relevant for grounding in chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) remain prone to visual hallucinations when correct responses depend on subtle visual details. One line of post-training methods constructs visual contrasts through image retrieval, generation, or editing to improve fine-grained perception. However, these contrasts are not built around the model's own hallucinations, making it difficult to ensure that supervision targets its actual perceptual confusions. Another line suppresses hallucinated outputs through response correction or preference learning under fixed image conditions, but leaves the design of supervision signals on the image-input side underexplored. Inspired by how humans correct misconceptions, we introduce DreamRealm, a post-training framework that explores diverse latent hallucinated responses, minimally edits original images to make them valid, and trains the model to assign each response to its supporting image. Our data construction pipeline, RealmForgePipe, mines observed and latent hallucinations from a target model and materializes them through image editing. Using hallucinations mined from Qwen3.5-9B, we construct the Realized Hallucination Dataset (RealmSet). Our training objective, RealmMatch, constructs a likelihood score matrix across image--response combinations and combines supervised learning with bidirectional contrastive losses to reinforce correct matches and suppress mismatches. Compared with the original Qwen3.5-9B model, DreamRealm improves performance across six visual perception and hallucination benchmarks, including gains of 7.26 percentage points in HallusionBench accuracy and 11.34 percentage points in MMVP pair accuracy. Together with ablation studies on data construction and training methods, these results demonstrate that hallucination assignment is a promising paradigm for improving visual perception and mitigating hallucinations.

### [Entropogram-guided Visual Realignment Decoding: Capturing the Rhythm of Multimodal Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06415)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CV` · 2611.06415

> Explores hallucination and reasoning dynamics in multimodal models, applicable to QA and RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have demonstrated remarkable progress on cross-modal reasoning tasks, yet they continue to suffer from hallucinations in long-chain reasoning processes. In this work, we revisit hallucination in MLLMs from the perspective of reasoning generation dynamics. We argue that entropy is not merely a static measure of uncertainty but a structured signal that reflects how modality information is integrated throughout decoding. Through systematic analysis, we identify two characteristic entropy patterns associated with hallucination behaviors: (1) a generally high-confidence regime corresponds to premature convergence to inappropriate multimodal interactions; and (2) local abrupt entropy transitions are associated with reasoning instability and hallucination onset. Building on these observations, we propose an entropy-aware self-introspective framework for MLLM reasoning. Our method continuously tracks entropy dynamics during decoding and performs targeted visual re-alignment when suspicious hallucination states are detected, encouraging the model to re-examine salient visual evidence at critical reasoning stages. Extensive experiments across six multimodal reasoning benchmarks spanning nine distinct evaluation settings demonstrate that our approach delivers robust improvements in reasoning accuracy and hallucination robustness, while providing new insights into the generation dynamics underlying MLLM failures. The code is available at: https://github.com/jden4524/SelfRetrospectiveDecoding.

</details>

### [SAUCE: Summary Analysis Using Conversation Entailment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06812)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.06812

> Proposes an evaluation pipeline for summarization which could support grounded generation.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

With the growing need for evaluating Large Language Models (LLMs) and their applications to speech, challenges persist in summarizing and evaluating conversations that lack a clear end goal. We introduce SAUCE -- a reference-free, fact-based evaluation pipeline for cross-lingual conversational speech summarization. It measures the accuracy and the fact coverage of a summary through the entailment between conversation and text. We compare SAUCE against several popular summarization metrics and demonstrate the effectiveness of capturing information loss due to transcription and translation error and identifying broken summaries. Crucially, unlike black-box LLM evaluators or dense embedding metrics, SAUCE is inherently explainable: it maps summary scores to discrete, verifiable facts, allowing users to pinpoint exact hallucinations or omissions. We illustrate how this interpretability helps developers systematically profile LLM behaviors and gives end-users an actionable tool to verify summary accuracy in noisy, real-world conditions. Preliminary investigations show SAUCE strongly align with human judgment.

### [Measuring Risk, Not Harm: Detecting Psychological Harm Risk Factors in LLM Conversations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06806)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.06806

> Relevant for guardrails in LLM interactions, focusing on psychological harm risks.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Psychological harms from interactions with large language models cannot be monitored directly because they may emerge over repeated interactions and depend strongly on subjective individual user context. We propose measuring risk, not harm: detecting observable AI behaviors that constitute risk factors for psychological harm at the AI response level and aggregating these signals to support longitudinal monitoring. We operationalize eight such behaviors and construct a synthetic multi-label corpus of 47,062 AI responses and 373,873 response-factor labels, with targeted human annotations for validation and evaluation. We then compare trained embedding-based classifiers, direct LLM judging, and a classifier--judge cascade. All detectors are model-agnostic and operate only on LLM outputs, requiring no access to internal model states. We apply these detectors to several out-of-distribution human-AI conversation data sets from prior research. On held-out human-labeled data, the best cascade achieves macro precision of 0.86, recall of 0.76, and F1 of 0.79. On WildChat, it reduces LLM-judge calls by 97.1\% relative to direct judging. On DelusionEval, risk-factor detections cluster within conversations. Together, our results show that response-level behavioral risk-factor detection can provide a scalable measurement layer for longitudinal psychological-safety monitoring without conflating observable AI behavior with latent user harm.

</details>

### [Twists and Turns: A Graph-Based Benchmark and Framework for Long-Term Memory in Exploration Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06414)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.06414

> Substantially relevant to long-term memory management and memory retrieval in processes, applicable to conversational agents.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Long-term problem-solving processes, such as scientific research and software development, proceed along an evolving trial-and-error trajectory toward a central goal. We term these \textit{exploration tasks}, which require models to remember not only facts but also how solutions are tested against intermediate outcomes and early attempts are continually revised or revived. However, existing long-term memory research focuses on factual recall and updates, overlooking the interconnected nature of exploration. We observe that exploration processes naturally form graphs, with branching approaches linked by revisions and reconsiderations. Motivated by this, we propose \textsc{LExMem}, a graph-guided benchmark designed to evaluate \underline{\textsc{Mem}}ory in \underline{\textsc{L}}ong-term \underline{\textsc{Ex}}ploration tasks. We model exploration processes as graphs and introduce an evolutionary mechanism to increase their structural complexity, yielding 224 dialogues and 2,018 question-answer instances derived from graph structures. Building on the same insight, we propose \textsc{LExGraph}, a graph-based memory framework that incrementally organizes dialogues into interconnected memory nodes and retrieves evidence through hybrid retrieval and neighbor expansion. Extensive experiments demonstrate that \textsc{LExMem} poses a significant challenge to existing retrieval- and memory-based systems, whereas \textsc{LExGraph} achieves superior performance and generalizes effectively to external benchmarks. Codes and datasets are available at https://github.com/mbzuai-nlp/LExMem.

### [Prospective Memory for Proactive LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05731)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.05731

> Relevant to conversation memory through proactive LLM memory strategies, worth skimming.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Most memory systems for LLM agents are retrospective, preserving and retrieving past information to inform current decisions. Proactive agents, however, also require prospective memory: retaining future-directed intentions and realizing them when appropriate opportunities arise, even without explicit requests. Crucially, remembering the right intention does not guarantee acting at the right time: a related observation may call for waiting, while a later cue may make the same intention actionable. Inspired by cognitive theories of human prospective memory, we introduce Pro-Memory, a memory framework that separates maintaining future intentions from deciding when and how to realize them. Pro-Memory preserves pending objectives linked to their supporting evidence, assesses new observations against their original goals and activation conditions, and coordinates appropriate contributions with the agent's ongoing actions. It further updates intention states based on actions actually issued or evidence of resolution, while an experience memory distills feedback from completed episodes into guidance for future decisions. Experiments on two benchmarks spanning delayed-intention tasks and open-ended proactive assistance demonstrate consistent improvements in proactive task performance over representative memory approaches across multiple LLM backbones, while limiting unnecessary interventions.

</details>

### [Signed but Steerable: Measuring and Closing the Text-Vector Integrity Gap in Offline Retrieval-Augmented Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06498)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.06498

> Addresses a critical issue of text-vector integrity in RAG, particularly for offline implementations.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Offline retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) on edge devices is updated through signed knowledge packs that carry text and precomputed embedding vectors. A signature proves who published a pack, not that its vectors encode its text. We study this text-vector integrity gap. A verifier that recomputes vectors and accepts them within a cosine tolerance concedes a predictable share of queries to an attacker who controls the vectors: the hijack rate equals the distribution of angular retrieval margins at the tolerance (the margin law). Margins are small in five corpora and two embedding models, so an idealised tolerance at the level of floating-point noise leaves up to 5.5% of queries exposed (at most 1.2% in any corpus once exact duplicate documents are excluded), and a threshold calibrated from our CPU INT8-FP32 comparison of a small embedding model (at the empirical 0.1% quantile of honest cosines) leaves 98-100% exposed; on a Raspberry Pi 5 running a local language model, such an attack lowers correct answers from 0.75 to 0.39. Integrity audits of compressed vector stores fare no better: on a FAISS product-quantisation index over 57,638 documents, tampered vectors that all pass the audit put the attacker's document first for 25% of targets. Verifying quantisation codes instead, with ambiguity decided by the verifier, a calibrated guard band and canonicalisation, accepted every honest vector in our samples on three platforms and left the publisher no choice. We also show that common floating-point and dynamic-INT8 inference pipelines are not bit-reproducible across batch sizes or architectures, and report pre-registered negative results on abstention, context pruning and semantic screening. We release code and data.

</details>

### [Optimal Re-Ranking Depth](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05908)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.05908

> Addresses optimal re-ranking depth which is relevant for retrieval processes in RAG systems.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Second-stage neural rankers are commonly applied with a fixed re-ranking depth, assuming that retrieval effectiveness saturates as depth increases. Prior research has questioned the assumption that increasing the re-ranking depth yields linear performance gains, further suggesting that optimal re-ranking depth varies considerably from query to query. In the past, studying such phenomena was methodologically difficult given the scale of manual annotation required. With the advent of LLM-based relevance judgments we can now more easily undertake such studies, in this case to pinpoint the optimal re-ranking depth on a per-query basis. Using dense LLM-based relevance judgments over a typical re-ranking pipeline, we show that many queries exhibit a well-defined optimal re-ranking depth, beyond which effectiveness stagnates or degrades. We formulate re-ranking depth as a query-specific property and study whether it can be predicted a priori from first-stage retrieval characteristics. Through a large-scale analysis, we find that most standard query performance prediction (QPP) methods are ineffective for this task. In contrast, a predictor derived from LLM-assessed first-stage ranking quality, which we term IR-DCG@10, can reduce the average re-ranking depth by up to a factor of 3 while preserving overall effectiveness, depending on the specific first- and second-stage rankers used. Under oracle selection of optimal depths, we further show that retrieval effectiveness can improve by more than 7% while reducing the average re-ranking depth by a factor of 5 on the MSMARCO DEV collection. Given our promising preliminary findings, we would encourage the use of automatic judgments to facilitate research otherwise infeasible under manual annotation. Towards this point, we release relevance judgments, our codebase, and experimental artefacts to support reproducibility and further research.

</details>
