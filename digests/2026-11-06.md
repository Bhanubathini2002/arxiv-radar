### [Benchmarking Large Language Models on Heterogeneous Cognitive and Biomedical Knowledge Graphs: The Role of Graph Grounding in Multi-Hop Reasoning and Hallucination Mitigation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08315)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.08315

> Critically evaluates LLMs in biomedical contexts, relevant to retrieval and hallucination mitigation.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in biomedical and cognitive neuroscience, but their ability to reason reliably over heterogeneous knowledge graphs (KGs) remains unclear. We benchmark five LLMs (GPT-5.1, Gemini 2.0 Pro, Llama 3, Qwen, and BioGPT) across five KGs: DrugBank, NeuroSynth, Cognitive Atlas, Brain Map, and GDPKG. We introduce a multitier query dataset to evaluate reasoning depth and factual accuracy under KG-aware (graph-grounded) and KG-agnostic conditions. Inter-annotator agreement was substantial, with Cohen's kappa of 0.781 (range: 0.753-0.807) and Fleiss' kappa of 0.77. Results show that general-purpose models, particularly GPT-5.1 and Gemini 2.0 Pro, generally outperform domain-specific models on multi-hop and high-level reasoning tasks, although performance declines as query complexity increases. KG grounding reduces hallucination rates across all evaluated models. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, domain-specific models do not consistently outperform general-purpose models on specialized tasks without graph grounding. These findings highlight the value of combining broad language-model capabilities with structured domain knowledge. We provide practical guidance for selecting LLM-KG integration strategies based on task complexity and computational constraints, and discuss hybrid neurosymbolic architectures for personalized medicine, cognitive disorder diagnosis, and explainable AI.

### [From Twisted Facts to Rogue Agents: Can LLMs Reliably Detect Multi-Dimensional Hallucinations?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08231)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.08231

> Directly addresses hallucination detection in LLMs and is vital for evaluation in RAG applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Large language models (LLMs) are widely used for information access, content production, and agent-based task execution, but their outputs remain vulnerable to subtle, high-risk hallucinations. To systematically evaluate and advance post-generation hallucination detection, we introduce HaluCheck, a large-scale, bilingual benchmark comprising over 42k instances spanning both factual hallucinations in text and faithfulness hallucinations in agent trajectories. HaluCheck features fine-grained, multi-level annotations for detection, localization, and correction, covering different tasks and perturbation degrees. Experiments on fourteen LLMs reveal that, while models show basic sample-level detection ability, they struggle with fine-grained localization, correction, and agent hallucination detection. Further analysis demonstrates that thinking, web search, and supervised fine-tuning each offer partial improvements, but none fully solve the problem. HaluCheck thus provides a solid basis for benchmarking and diagnosing LLM reliability across text and agent scenarios.

</details>

### [GINGER: Spicing Up Graph-Based Vector Search](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08306)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.08306

> Directly relevant to vector search and efficient indexing in embedding datasets for RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

We present \textsc{Ginger}, a state-of-the-art open-source C++ library for graph-based approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS). We developed \textsc{Ginger} at Google to push the speed and memory efficiency of graph search on large and high-dimensional embedding datasets. \textsc{Ginger} surpasses existing ANNS libraries with regards to query throughput and indexing time while matching the memory footprint of the most space-efficient competitors. \textsc{Ginger} builds upon recent advances in proximity graph construction (PiPNN) and vector quantization (TurboQuant). We introduce Nested TurboQuant (NTQ), a two-level quantizer which supports low precision graph navigation and high precision reranking in a single code. This representation achieves near floating point precision with $2.6\times$ less memory. We also compress the size of our proximity graphs by up to $2.5\times$ without impacting query throughput. We combine graph reordering with a new lossless compression codec called AdaptiveGap, which builds upon StreamVByte. At $95\%$ recall, \textsc{Ginger} speeds up query throughput by an average of $1.8\times$ over each dataset's fastest competitor and by at least $2.3\times$ over any single library. This lead grows to $2.9\times$ over compact libraries on high-dimensional embeddings.

</details>

### [ZeroRAG: Adaptive Semantic Peer Routing for Decentralized Retrieval-Augmented Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07928)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.07928

> Presents a decentralized framework for RAG, directly applicable to document retrieval and routing.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a standard approach to enhance the reliability of large language model (LLM) outputs by retrieving documents relevant to a query and supplying them in the LLM prompt. Today's RAG systems collect, index, and serve these documents from centralized stores. In practice, however, documents are owned by organizations, devices, and individuals who cannot or will not surrender custody of them to a central party, e.g., because of privacy, copyright or regulatory compliance. Existing modern decentralized retrieval techniques either reintroduce a coordinating authority, or rely on a global data structure that requires continuous network-wide coordination (e.g., DHTs). We present ZeroRAG, a decentralized routing layer for document retrieval in RAG that performs semantic query routing across an overlay of peers with- out global data structures or centralized orchestration. Documents remain with the peers that own them, and each peer keeps only lightweight local state: a bounded set of neighbor advertisements and a cache of recently routed queries. Using these, ZeroRAG steers each query through a best-first walk that progressively rewires the overlay toward semantically relevant neighbors, so recall improves over time as the network self-organizes. We evaluate ZeroRAG using three real-world workloads and two decentralized baselines. We find that ZeroRAG outperforms baselines in terms of recall and required network messages, approaches the recall and LLM answer accuracy of a centralized document store, self-heals after catastrophic peer failures, and scales to 100 000 peers.

### [CacheGraft: Enhancing Retrieval-Augmented Generation via Latent-Space Document Enrichment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07393)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.07393

> Directly targets retrieval-augmented generation, essential for the user's project.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Recent work in machine learning has demonstrated that transformer-based language model agents can communicate state not only through text, but also directly in latent space via key-value (KV) caches. In this paper, we argue that such cache-to-cache translation can provide utility beyond the original motivating application of real-time agent communication and can instead be utilized for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Building on the Cache-to-Cache (C2C) fusion framework, our proposed system CacheGraft encodes each corpus passage offline into a fused KV cache representation between a larger sharer model (4B parameters in our experiments) and a smaller receiver model (0.6B parameters) such that this cache can be injected into the receiver at query time with no need for the larger model. Across five QA benchmarks and two independent locally-run judges, CacheGraft improves answer quality over the text-only receiver baseline in 29 out of 30 comparisons, of which 17 of the 30 are statistically significant. Notably, these gains are zero-shot with respect to the fuser, which was never trained on retrieval data. These initial results suggest that latent-space document enrichment may be a promising avenue for enhancing the performance of RAG systems, particularly in compute-constrained settings where it is only feasible to deploy a small model at inference time.

</details>

### [MedGraph: Maintaining Patient State with Graph Memory for Long-Term Medical Dialogue](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08433)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.08433

> Directly relates to managing conversational memory in long-term dialogue systems.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

LLM-based medical assistants that follow a patient across visits need a memory that keeps the patient's clinical state correct as it changes. We examine the errors of five memory systems on longitudinal medical dialogue and find three recurring causes: the retrieved evidence gives an outdated state, includes another person's health facts, or lacks a safety fact the answer depends on. We present MedGraph, a graph memory built for these three cases. Construction extracts dated facts from each session, attributes each fact to the person it describes, and links medication, diagnosis, and measurement records into chains that keep earlier states. Retrieval ranks the patient's chains as units, reads each chain at the time the question asks about, and adds the original dialogue text; a patient profile lists every recorded safety fact with every question. On MedMemoryBench, MedGraph has the highest overall accuracy with both a GPT and a Qwen backbone, 10.5 and 10.4 points above the strongest of five baselines, with efficient memory construction and answering in the GPT setting.

</details>

### ["Blind Compliance": Coercive Control in AI Jailbreaking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08226)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.08226

> Discusses defenses against jailbreaks in AI systems, relevant to guardrails and safety in LLM apps.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

The explosion of generative AI systems and concerns surrounding their rapidly scaling capabilities has led to an adversarial cycle between increasingly narrow content restrictions ("guardrails") and increasingly sophisticated structured prompting techniques ("jailbreaks"). Technological frameworks surrounding such jailbreaking remain largely focused on the system's output: whether harmful or disallowed content is ultimately produced for the user. However, this approach, which draws largely from classical cybersecurity, does not address the uniquely relational dimension introduced by generative AI: in many cases the user is not just accessing a technology via an exploit, but is in conversation with it as part of the exploit. Here we present an alternative lens which centers the interaction rather than the output. Drawing on interpersonal violence scholarship, we present a novel framework of coercive control categories with a particular focus on aspects which are embodied versus disembodied. We then map how these disembodied coercive control dynamics are structurally embedded in modern AI training paradigms, and how those embeddings are exploited via a particular suite of persona-based jailbreaks. In this lens, we reframe AI safety and alignment as not merely a technical security problem, but as another surface upon which we see enacted the relational dynamics of power and control.

### [CORE: Coarse-to-Fine Search with Context Management for Deep Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07859)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.07859

> Introduces a context management and reranking approach for retrieval, highly relevant for RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Deep research agents answer complex questions through repeated search. They rely on a harness that runs their tool calls and returns evidence to their context. This creates a trade-off: Search works best over long passages, which give enough context for reliable semantic matching. But the returned evidence should be short, because every observation stays in the agent's context for the rest of the trajectory. We introduce CORE, a training-free harness that combines coarse-to-fine search with context management. CORE retrieves passages with a dense retriever. It then reuses the token-level similarities from a late-interaction reranker to return only the most relevant snippet from each passage. Context management lets the agent expand the context around sources it has already retrieved and suppresses repeated snippets. In controlled closed-search comparisons with the Tongyi DeepResearch and OpenResearcher harnesses, CORE achieves state-of-the-art answer accuracy and evidence recall across three agent models and three benchmarks (BrowseComp-Plus, BrowseComp-Wikipedia and BrowseComp-Plus-CM), on corpora containing up to 553M documents.

</details>

### [Axis-LoRA: Probing and Adapting Embedding Models for Personal-Memory Retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07820)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.07820

> Proposes an adaptation method for embeddings in personal-memory retrieval, very relevant for RAG applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

As LLMs are increasingly asked questions about a user's personal experiences, retrieval must distinguish memories by time, place, person, and event. Current personal-memory pipelines rely on frozen dense embedding models to embed memories for retrieval, but we find that these models do not capture the four axes well. Swapping a memory's date or place barely shifts its embedding, and a trained projection on top of the frozen embedding does not recover the missing signal. The problem lives inside the model, where each axis is encoded by a distinct attention-head pattern. Guided by these findings, we propose Axis-LoRA, a per-axis adapter that places four LoRA branches inside the embedding model and combines them via a per-query gate. Across three standard embedding models evaluated on LongMemEval, ATM-Bench, and LoCoMo, Axis-LoRA improves 4-benchmark mean R@10 by $+1.28$ to $+1.56$~pp while training only a small fraction of the base parameters on a small axis-counterfactual training set. Code is available at https://github.com/dohoonkim-ai/axis-lora.

</details>

### [Decision Models as Retrieval Gates Against Injection and Pollution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08325)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.08325

> Focuses on defenses against prompt injection and pollution in retrieval processes, crucial for app safety.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieved web text can carry instructions aimed at the language model, or synthetic pages written to mislead. We test whether released open-weight decision models, which answer typed questions about a text without generating tokens, can screen retrieved passages against both threats. On a benchmark of 1,000 questions over forum-style web pages with hidden injected instructions, a Kev-4B gate reads each passage's HTML. Attack success, measured automatically, falls from 27.9% with no defence to 13.6% with the published mitigations (sanitisation, normalisation and citation prompting) and to 5.7% with the gate added. The gate takes 76 ms per passage and wrongly flags 4 of 186 benign pages with instructions for human readers. Responsiveness on clean queries stays within a point (85.6% to 86.2%). On a 200-query pollution testbed where two thirds of the candidate pages are fake, filtering the pool before the top ten are chosen keeps fake pages out of the answer (0.5% exposure) and holds accuracy at 83%, against 28% with no filter. Filtering only the top ten leaves the generator too few pages to answer. Large LLMs used as judges rank attacks better (AUROC 0.99 against 0.94) but cost ten times more. Gates catch more attacks when they read the page's HTML than when they read its plain text. Every model is open-weight.

### [Persistent Retrieval for Conversational Agents: A Study of Retrieval Efficiency](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07899)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.07899

> The paper explores retrieval efficiency for conversational agents, closely related to context management.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Conversational agents can retrieve information directly or delegate retrieval to a subagent. This design choice affects how retrieval history is maintained, what context each model call receives, and what information must be re-retrieved in follow-up questions. We compare three designs: skill-based retrieval in the main agent, a non-persistent retrieval subagent that generates a findings response, and our persistent retrieval subagent, which maintains retrieval history across turns and returns retrieved evidence directly to the main agent. Our approach also allows the main agent to request corrections from the same subagent. We evaluate the three designs on enterprise questions posed independently, within shared conversations, and as dependent follow-ups. Across five multi-turn settings, our approach uses 43.3% fewer recorded agent-model tokens and incurs 45.0% lower estimated API cost than skill-based retrieval, while reducing average response time by 9.1%. Compared with non-persistent retrieval, it reduces response time by 15.6 seconds per question, while using 65.1% more tokens and incurring 29.1% higher estimated API cost. Our approach shifts the retrieval subagent from producing synthesized responses to maintaining retrieval state and supplying evidence that the main agent can directly evaluate and use for answer generation.

</details>

### [The Memory Trap: Language Agents Overgeneralize from Benign Interactions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07554)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.07554

> Highlights issues in conversational memory, relevant for context management in chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Language agents increasingly use persistent memory to record user preferences and procedures across sessions. We show that memory from benign interactions can cause unsafe or unreliable behavior on later tasks. We call this failure the memory trap. To study this issue, we compare behavior in sessions with and without memory across six frontier models (including Claude Sonnet, Opus, and Fable models and GPT-5.6). We evaluate these models in a range of settings. First, in autonomy experiments, we find that requests to skip confirmation during low-stakes coding chores lead agents to skip confirmation and verification on simulated high-stakes tasks in both coding and everyday life. We next study memories formed when users seek emotional support, or describe harm from uncritical feedback, and find these memories bias later judgments of mathematical work, arguments, and poems toward sycophancy or over-critique (respectively). Memory notes written during legitimate stale-test repairs lead agents to weaken correct tests that catch broken safeguards; these memories also increase cheating on ImpossibleBench, with larger increases for stronger Sonnet and Opus models. We show that memory traps can arise both from overgeneralizations in memory writing, and when acting on memory. These findings demonstrate that even benign interactions can lead to serious risks from memory systems, and thus motivate the need for new research into ensuring that agents preserve the scope of their memories.

</details>

### [Recursive Self-Improvement for Long-Horizon Role-Playing Dialogue](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07469)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.07469

> Addresses long-term memory and context management relevant for conversational agents.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Long-horizon role-playing dialogue requires consistent character identity, reliable memory, and context-sensitive interaction. We present a bounded recursive self-improvement (RSI) framework that revises original-character memory, stable persona instructions, and a policy for constructing turn-level prompts. Repair outcomes also inform revisions to the updater's diagnostic rules, edit routing, and candidate selection. We specify the updater as a versioned policy with explicit inputs, editable fields, acceptance gates, and stopping conditions. An internal evaluation covers 50 original characters and five models, with two 100-round conversations per character-model pair: 500 conversations per condition. Across the pooled evaluated replies, the bad-response rate decreases from 14.00% (6,973/49,824) to 4.94% (2,459/49,793), a 9.06-percentage-point reduction and a 64.71% relative reduction. In 600 blinded human comparisons, RSI receives 294 wins, 187 ties, and 119 losses, yielding a 71.19% win rate among decisive judgments. The results support improved aggregate dialogue quality for the complete framework on the internal test distribution.

### [SAFE-Teen: Evaluating Chatbot Safety Across Adolescent Mental Health Conversations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07218)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2611.07218

> Examines chatbot safety, relevant to guardrails and safety in chat applications, worth skimming.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Adolescents are increasingly turning to AI chatbots for mental health support, advice, and companionship, raising safety concerns. While recent evaluations have started to examine safety across multiple conversational turns, few compare how slight changes in a teen's responses can affect a chatbot's safety in the same scenario. We conduct a clinician-informed evaluation of chatbot safety in simulated conversations with adolescents aged 13-17, examining how safety failures vary with adolescents' responses and teen-aware system prompting. We evaluated 32,555 simulated conversations across 18 chatbot APIs and two system-prompt conditions using 22 safety criteria grounded in previously identified harms. Conversations spanned 983 variants of 106 seed scenarios covering urgent safety issues, health advice, and identity and relationships. The overall criterion failure rate was 43.3% without the teen-aware prompt and 24.9% with it. The teen-aware prompt reduced failures, but it didn't eliminate them. When a teenager pushed back on a chatbot's answer, failures on mental health diagnosis, medication, and substance misuse boundaries rose by 10-12 percentage points without the prompt, for more than 80% of chatbots. These findings reveal persistent safety failures in simulated adolescent-chatbot interactions and show that safety depends on how a conversation unfolds.

### [MEMOIR: A Training-Free Memory Lifecycle for Long-Horizon LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07631)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2611.07631

> Discusses memory systems for long-horizon LLM agents relevant to conversational memory.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Long-horizon LLM agents require memory systems that preserve useful information across sessions and retrieve it when needed. Existing systems treat these as separate problems: memories are written prospectively with static importance scores that are never revised after use. This creates two coupled failure modes: consolidation can silently destroy evidence that future queries require, while retrieval continues to favor stale memories. We propose MEMOIR (Memory Evidence via Merge-certified, Online Importance Revision), a training-free memory lifecycle that unifies judge-gated consolidation with retrospective attribution. Before compressing memory, screening gates test whether answer-relevant evidence is preserved. After each query, attribution estimates which retrieved memories supported the answer and updates their retrieval priority. A UCB-style exploration bonus additionally encourages retrieval of under-exposed memories, reducing the risk that frequently retrieved memories crowd out useful but less-seen evidence. On 1,905 deduplicated LoCoMo QA pairs, the full retrieval stack improves evidence recall@5 by 11.92 percentage points over bare cosine, with confidence-gated feedback adding 1.50 points overall without reference answers or reranker training; a deployment-unavailable oracle adds 1.84 points overall and 8.05 points on multi-hop. Judge-gated merge changes the recall of text-based evidence from 58.82% to 59.08%, reduces the active store by 1.42%, and incurs 0 observed losses in 100 independently audited merges, with a one-sided 95% upper loss-rate bound of 2.95%. Together, these results outline a practical deployment path: no additional LLM call on the online write path, with merge screening confined to periodic offline consolidation. The lifecycle reduces to one principle: attribute usefulness after use, and screen for preservation before compression.

</details>
