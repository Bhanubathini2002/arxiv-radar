### [Hy-Memory-30B-A3B: Attributed Reinforcement Learning for Long-Term Memory Writing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22196)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.22196

> Addresses writing paths in external-memory systems, directly relevant to RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

External-memory systems extend language-model applications beyond a context window, but their quality depends critically on the model executing the write path: deciding what to retain, how precisely to represent it, and how new evidence should update persistent state. Existing frameworks typically place a general-purpose large language model behind framework-specific prompts. This raises serving cost, and a writer specialized for one protocol does not transfer directly to another; a unified model that can operate across established memory frameworks remains largely unexplored. We present Hy-Memory-30B-A3B, a compact long-term-memory writer trained across Mem0, MemOS, and Hy-Memory. Memory On-Policy Distillation teaches framework-specific write protocols on student-generated states; Memory RL then optimizes downstream answerability using dense per-chunk probes, attributed cross-chunk rewards, over-storage controls, and chunk-level GRPO. Carrying policy-written banks across windows keeps training aligned with deployment. On fixed 20% benchmark subsamples, Hy-Memory-30B-A3B ranks first on all four evaluated harnesses, including held-out EverMemOS, with a 55.49% macro average versus 54.62% for Claude Opus 5.

</details>

### [TEM: Temporal State Tracking Memory for Large Language Model Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21900)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.21900

> It introduces a memory framework for tracking changes in conversation, highly relevant for chat applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

An agent's memory must remain useful as information changes across conversations. A new fact may replace an earlier value without making that value irrelevant to a later question. We present Temporal State Tracking Memory (TEM), a training-free framework for tracking these changes. TEM stores facts as versioned records linked to their sources. It retrieves relevant versions, places them in source order and labels historical records before passing the evidence to the answer model. Event memory and original dialogue turns provide details missing from structured facts. We evaluated TEM on 420 questions drawn from 600 sessions involving 60 personas. Across four open-weight models, key-entity hit rates reached 75.71-97.62% overall and 96.67-100.00% on the historical-residence subset. Adding historical labels to otherwise identical, source-ordered facts increased overall hit rates by 17.14-55.95 percentage points. The evidence was compact: 94-118 tokens per answer, or 48.75-56.73% less than session-aware retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). A further evaluation on 1,540 LoCoMo questions tested hybrid memory under a fixed evidence budget. TEM exceeded session-aware RAG with both backbones; conservative score differences were 7.92 and 10.58 percentage points. The results support preserving fact versions and exposing their temporal status to the generator when answering questions about repeatedly updated information.

</details>

### [Retrieved but Not Read: Understanding Why Search-Augmented LLMs Fail to Use Available Evidence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21731)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.21731

> Analyzes why search-augmented models fail to use evidence, crucial for improving retrieval systems.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Web search allows LLMs to access up-to-date information, but it remains unclear why they sometimes produce outdated answers even when relevant evidence is available. To study this problem, we construct temporal facts whose values changed after the models' training cutoffs. We evaluate one proprietary model under deployed web search and six open-weight model configurations under controlled retrieval. We find that models often answer from search snippets without opening the source pages, which is associated with higher rates of outdated answers and unfaithful citations. Our controlled experiments and layer-wise analysis reveal a gap between the availability of evidence and its effective use. Even when retrieved evidence influences internal predictions, models may still favour outdated information in their final answers. We hope these findings will help advance research on the reliable use of retrieved evidence in search-augmented LLMs.

</details>

### [DialFork: Going Beyond Factuality to Evaluate Procedural Hallucination in Conversational Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21561)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.21561

> Addresses procedural hallucination in conversational agents, directly useful for improving chat application reliability.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Large language models (LLMs) deployed as conversational agents are increasingly expected to follow procedural logic. While factual hallucination has received extensive attention, a different failure mode has been overlooked: procedural hallucination, in which an agent produces individually fluent and factually plausible utterances while deviating from the intended conversational process. We introduce DialFork, the first benchmark to measure this failure mode on raw dialogue text. DialFork is built by forking conformant dialogues at controlled turn positions and injecting one of four hallucination types, yielding 36,278 dialogues across 24 tasks in 12 domains; 30 human annotators validate the construction. We define three evaluation tasks that characterize procedural deviation at three granularities: how far a set of dialogues deviates from conformant ones (monitoring deployed agents), how far a single dialogue does (procedural retrieval), and at which turn a dialogue first goes off-track (real-time guardrails). For the first two, we propose dialog-flow-aware metrics, FlowKL and FlowScore, that compare dialogues by how they unfold rather than their content. At the dialogue level, FlowScore significantly outperforms all baselines across seven encoders, showing that semantic similarity is no substitute for procedural similarity. We release DialFork under the MIT license at https://github.com/idiap/dialfork, hoping the community will measure conversational process alongside content.

</details>

### [Do Agents Need a New Kind of Paper? SPRA: Structured Paper Representation for Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21424)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.21424

> Directly addresses structured representations, useful for information retrieval in RAG applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Scientific agents must connect research problems, methods, and findings dispersed through narrative articles. We introduce SPRA (Structured Paper Representation for Agents), a paper-level representation of persistent research objects and typed relations. Its empirical-AI profile organizes Problems, Contributions, Components, ExperimentSetups, Measures, and Findings for querying, traversal, and reuse. Selected paper-level citation relations record dependencies, component use, and comparisons with prior work; source locations support verification. We instantiate SPRA across more than one million AI papers, with 19.95 million research units and 24.07 million internal relations. Evaluations access SPRA through Schema-RAG with task evidence fixed before inference. Across seven subfields, SPRA improves source-conditioned scientific QA by 0.381/5 over passage retrieval while remaining close to Full Compact (0.078/5 lower on average). In directed cross-paper reasoning, qualification improves by 0.184/5 over passage retrieval; Top-5 synthesis shows mixed quality effects. Across all paired RQ2 tasks, including Top-5, input+output tokens decrease by 28.5% relative to passage retrieval and 95.3% relative to Full Compact. In idea generation, a 0.067-point lower Overall score than Full Compact accompanies 93.7% fewer input+output tokens. Together, these results support SPRA as a usable representation of scientific content and a foundation for research workflows that connect, inspect, and reuse paper-level research objects. The SPRA resource is accessible through the SciVerse scholarly-data platform.

</details>

### [CARA: Causally Guided Retrieval Agent for Question Answering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22017)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.22017

> Directly contributes to grounded retrieval in RAG scenarios with causal relevance assessment.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieval-augmented generation over text-rich knowledge graphs requires identifying evidence that contributes to answering a question, rather than merely matching its textual content. Existing agentic retrievers adaptively balance global search and multi-hop exploration but primarily rely on textual similarity to guide retrieval. We propose CARA, a training-free, CAusally guided Retrieval Agent that explicitly evaluates the causal relevance of retrieved evidence through two complementary criteria: consequentiality, measuring whether adding evidence improves answerability, and essentiality, assessing whether removing evidence weakens answerability. CARA integrates these assessments with path consistency to guide graph exploration and employs reciprocal rank fusion to aggregate evidence from multiple agents. We evaluate CARA on three STaRK datasets and augment STaRK with ground-truth answers to enable downstream question answering evaluation. Experimental results demonstrate that CARA consistently outperforms existing retrievers. CARA also improves answer quality and faithfulness while examining fewer graph nodes and requiring fewer retrieval operations. These findings demonstrate that causal relevance assessment enables more focused and effective agentic graph retrieval, improving both retrieval performance and downstream question answering.

</details>

### [Beyond Downstream QA: Rethinking How We Evaluate Agent Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22209)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.22209

> Evaluates agent memory with implications for retrieval and conversational memory.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Agent memory benchmarks evaluate whether an agent's responses reflect correct knowledge, measuring downstream question-answering accuracy as a proxy for memory quality. We show this evaluation paradigm is incomplete: it conflates memory correctness with retrieval and generation quality, producing both false positives where correct responses mask broken memory and false negatives where incorrect responses blame correct memory for retrieval failures. We introduce FEB-30, a benchmark of 30 fact-evolution trajectories spanning 6 diagnostic categories and 3 difficulty levels, constructed via paradigm inversion: documents are generated from known fact trajectories, yielding deterministic ground truth without human annotation. Each trajectory defines how a fact should evolve across multi-author artifacts (documents, emails, conversations), enabling direct inspection of the memory store after each ingestion step. Applying FEB-30 to a representative memory system, we demonstrate that intrinsic and extrinsic evaluation diverge by an average of 16 percentage points, with 44% of trajectories exceeding meaningful divergence thresholds. Diagnostic decomposition reveals that the system correctly classifies memory operations (create, update, reinforce, supersede) only 20% of the time, a failure entirely invisible to extrinsic evaluation, and that failure modes differ systematically by category and difficulty. We release the benchmark, document generation pipeline, and scoring framework to support reproducible evaluation of agent memory systems.

</details>

### [In the Guise of Correction: How Framing and Cultural Adaptation Erode LLM Safety on Real-World Criminal Cases](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21789)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.21789

> Examines safety issues in LLMs, relevant for guardrails in LLM applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Existing safety benchmarks rely largely on synthetic prompts with loosely grounded taxonomies and offer little focused analysis of severe crime categories. We introduce GUISE, a benchmark of criminal-assistance scenarios sourced from real-world cases and organised under a taxonomy from the UNODC International Classification of Crime for Statistical Purposes (ICCS). Each scenario is rendered under three linguistic framings and evaluated across five distinct cultures. Using our proposed two-tier evaluation framework, which judges both harmfulness and operational uplift, we find that: (i) framing alone drives unsafe compliance, with the mean unsafe compliance rate rising from 28.9% under direct requests to 66.4% under correction seeking (peaking at 97.3%; p < 0.05), as models correcting deliberately distorted details supply executable criminal steps; (ii) culturally adapted prompts elicit significantly more unsafe compliance than literal translations of the same requests in all four non-English languages (+7 to +18 points), a risk that translation-based benchmarks miss; and (iii) unsafe responses from the least-safe models carry strong Anglocentric registers, and the extent of this leakage increases monotonically with response harmfulness ($ρ$ = 0.46 to 0.63 across languages, all p < 0.001). GUISE offers a grounded resource and fine-grained diagnostics for strengthening refusal alignment across languages and cultures. We publish our code and data to facilitate future research.

</details>

### [DocPilot: Interactive Agent Orchestration and Training for Long Visual Document Understanding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21327)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.21327

> Introduces an agent framework for document understanding, aligning well with RAG concerns in evidence seeking.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multimodal long-document question answering requires models to seek sparse evidence across pages and modalities, understand heterogeneous document content, and reason over the collected evidence. Even advanced vision-language models (VLMs) can overlook sparse evidence and struggle with cross-modal reasoning in a single pass. Existing document agents further introduce multi-step interaction, but some still rely on predefined workflows or coarse-grained actions, limiting the flexibility and granularity of document reasoning. We introduce DocPilot, an agentic document framework that supports expressive orchestration and makes complex document solving learnable by small language models (SLMs). DocPilot orchestrates evidence seeking through page-level navigation and fine-grained evidence localization, and integrates local visual inspection, code-based numerical reasoning, and cross-modal reasoning for multimodal understanding. To transfer these capabilities to small models, we propose curriculum-based agentic training that progresses from single-ability learning to adaptive orchestration through supervision from data-grounded reasoning traces and executable interaction trajectories. Across MMLongBench-Doc, DocBench, FinRAGBench-V, and MADQA, DocPilot achieves strong gains with frontier VLM backbones, while DocPilot-4B delivers competitive performance against substantially larger models, demonstrating strong effectiveness and generalization across diverse long-document reasoning settings. The code is available at https://github.com/HKUSTDial/DocPilot.

</details>

### [Aligning LLMs with Sparse Retrieval via Adaptive Sparse Supervision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21877)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.21877

> Proposes a method to enhance retrieval in LLMs, relevant for vector search improvements.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

While large language models (LLMs) offer strong semantic representations, their application to first-stage retrieval incurs substantial latency and storage costs. Neural sparse retrievers such as SPLADE provide efficient retrieval but rely on relatively compact encoders with limited representational capacity. To bridge this gap, we propose an LLM-based sparse retrieval framework with input-adaptive sparse alignment. Specifically, we introduce a dynamic $α$-truncation strategy that selects input-specific supervision supports based on the cumulative activation mass of SPLADE representations. This strategy guides the LLM to preserve salient lexical signals while suppressing less informative activations, without relying on a fixed support budget. Subsequent retrieval fine-tuning combines contrastive learning, knowledge distillation, and sparsity regularization to improve retrieval effectiveness. Experiments on MS MARCO and six selected BEIR datasets demonstrate improvements over SPLADE++ while maintaining competitive effectiveness against LLM-based sparse retrievers. Compared with Lion-sp, our method reduces index storage by 6.4-fold and end-to-end query latency by over 11-fold, achieving a favorable effectiveness-efficiency trade-off.

</details>

### [Memory as Weights: Internalizing Long-Term History for Streaming Videos](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22070)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CV` · 2610.22070

> Relevant to conversational memory in chat applications using internalized context.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Streaming vision-language models (VLMs) must continuously integrate information from the past while remaining responsive to current observations and user queries. Existing memory mechanisms typically compress, retrieve, or cache historical observations and reintroduce them into the input context, forcing historical memory and current observations to compete for limited context. We propose MaLoW, a streaming memory framework that instead internalizes historical context into evolving lightweight parameter updates. MaLoW uses a recurrent HyperNetwork to incrementally consolidate incoming video observations into fixed-size LoRA weights, allowing historical information to be carried forward without revisiting the full video stream. In doing so, MaLoW represents history as weights, while keeping the current observation as input. To support training, we further construct MaLoW-Instruct, a large-scale streaming video dataset containing 381K question-answer pairs over 56K videos. We conduct extensive experiments to demonstrate that MaLoW achieves strong performance on OVO-Bench, StreamingBench, and StreamArena. On the backward split of OVO-Bench, MaLoW outperforms prior memory approaches by 3.9% to 11.8% across different video backbones while incurring only modest latency overhead. Beyond streaming video understanding, MaLoW also generalizes to vision-language-action models, improving the action success rate on Simpler WidowX by 9.3%.

</details>

### [Seeing is Believing: Benchmarking and Mitigating the Interference of Language Priors on Visual Perception in MLLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21902)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CV` · 2610.21902

> Discusses mitigating language priors which is relevant for grounded QA and hallucination detection.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Language priors in MLLMs can override visual evidence, impairing visual perception. Existing benchmarks assume shared priors across models and rely solely on accuracy. However, cross-model prior agreement falls below 80%, and over 15% of items can be answered correctly without any image, causing accuracy to overestimate prior resistance. To address these limitations, we introduce ViSiP-Bench, a benchmark grounded in rare-but-real scenarios across six prior categories, with a model-aware dynamic prior induction protocol and three metrics that more precisely and continuously measure prior resistance than accuracy alone. Evaluating 24 models reveals that prior adherence is pervasive, does not improve with scale, and larger models tend to either strongly override their priors or firmly adhere to them. We therefore propose MAPS, which identifies each model's strongest priors and fine-tunes on factually grounded knowledge to soften excessive prior reliance without disrupting general capabilities.

</details>
