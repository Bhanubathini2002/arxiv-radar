### [EpiLens: Lightweight RAG for Epidemiological Knowledge Acquisition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26730)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.26730

> Develops a RAG framework for epidemiological knowledge, directly applicable to user goals.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

To prepare for future outbreaks, researchers need to know what studies have been done and what data they use. Mapping a large literature by hand is slow, while large language models alone can misstate or conflate sources. We present EpiLens, a lightweight, open-source retrieval-augmented generation package that applies a fixed retrieval template and output schema to every paper in a corpus and links outputs to source passages. We build a corpus of 2,297 papers from the sources of a multi-pathogen parameter study and evaluate EpiLens's paper-classification workflow against 214 manually labelled papers. Without task-specific training data, the classifier matches or exceeds the tested supervised and zero-shot baselines across four tasks. The study's early extraction sheets cite published sources for 327 estimates. We link each estimate to a cited document or to corpus studies included by a review. Then, we use the classifier's labels to compare the papers associated with different parameters and pathogens. EpiLens labels more papers linked to reproduction numbers than fatality risks as model-based (54% versus 22%, compared with 62% versus 24% in the manual labels). The gap is consistent with the frequent use of transmission models to estimate reproduction numbers and persists within each of five pathogens compared. Beyond the contrast between parameters, the model-based share of papers linked to incubation-period estimates differs by pathogen: 55% for SARS-CoV-2 versus 6% for mpox.

### [VisR-RAG-Fin: Benchmarking Multimodal RAG over Visually Rich Financial Documents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24822)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.24822

> Comprehensively studies multimodal RAG applied to complex financial documents, highly relevant.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Enterprise documents such as financial reports are increasingly processed by automated systems, yet they are primarily designed for human reading and exhibit complex visual structure, combining dense text with tables, charts, diagrams, and rich layouts. These characteristics pose significant challenges for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, which must retrieve relevant pages and leverage visual and structural information during answer generation. Despite recent advances in multimodal retrieval and vision-language models, there remains a limited understanding of how design choices across retrieval and generation components affect end-to-end RAG performance on visually rich documents. In this paper, we present a comprehensive empirical study of multimodal RAG for page-level Question Answering (QA) over financial documents. We introduce VisR-RAG-Fin (Visually Rich RAG for Finance), an open-source benchmark constructed through a general pipeline for visually rich documents that preserves original layout and visual content. Using this benchmark, we systematically analyze retrieval and generation behavior under text-only, multimodal, and hybrid configurations, providing a foundation for rigorous evaluation of RAG systems on complex enterprise document collections. Overall, this work provides actionable insights for practitioners designing and evaluating multimodal RAG pipelines over complex, visually rich enterprise documents.

### [RAGit: Turning Document Preprocessing into an Experimental Variable for RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24659)
**Score 10/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.24659

> Directly addresses document preprocessing impacts on RAG systems, which is critical for effective implementation.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Document preprocessing choices can substantially affect retrieval in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipelines, yet they are often selected heuristically and are difficult to compare under consistent conditions. We present RAGit, an open-source experimentation workbench for the systematic and reproducible evaluation of document preprocessing choices through downstream retrieval. RAGit represents parsing, chunking, indexing, retrieval, and evaluation as configurable experimental stages, enabling controlled comparisons while the remaining downstream settings are kept fixed. It preserves provenance from retrieved chunks to source pages and maps heterogeneous retrieval units to a common page-level ranking for evaluation. The current implementation supports multiple parsers and chunking strategies, dense and late-interaction retrieval, reusable intermediate artifacts, and multi-configuration benchmarking. We demonstrate RAGit through controlled experiments comparing alternative preprocessing configurations under a fixed retrieval setup, illustrating how preprocessing can be treated as an explicit experimental variable. RAGit and its documentation are publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/OmarElbachyr/ragit). A demonstration video is available at https://youtu.be/tsst-Bhfz4I.

### [KEEP-Bench: Evaluating Evidence Preservation in Context Compression](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26827)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.26827

> Evaluates compression faithfulness and evidence preservation, very relevant to RAG evaluation metrics.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Context compression improves long-context efficiency but may introduce faithfulness issues through an information bottleneck. Existing evaluations rely on QA metrics, but a reader can still answer correctly from an unfaithful compression, so these metrics underestimate such loss. To bridge this gap, we introduce KEEP-Bench, a diagnostic benchmark that directly assesses compression faithfulness with 4,102 cases across three challenging settings that require keeping evidence for plausible reference resolutions, explicit qualifiers, and minority perspectives. Experiments show that even with tailored prompting, the strongest models remain unreliable: despite context windows exceeding 100K tokens and inputs averaging only 3.1K tokens, the best open-weight model (31B) preserves all required evidence in only 66.8% of cases, and the best proprietary model in only 61.6%. Performance also varies sharply across evidence structures, so conclusions from limited structures may be biased. We further introduce a metric, Evidence Preservation Return (EPR), to relate evidence recall to inference FLOPs, and find that it peaks at moderate scales (4B-8B), where recall remains low. Additional output budget mainly benefits already strong models and widens capability gaps, yet the stronger preservation of these models comes at a steep computational cost. These findings highlight the importance for future work to investigate compressors with strong information preservation under a small computation budget, rather than relying on a large output budget or model scaling.

</details>

### [RESIN: Multi-Chain Reasoning with Entity-Structured Indexing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26782)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.26782

> Directly addresses multi-hop retrieval and evidence organization for RAG applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Multi-chain reasoning is a challenging task not only because the multi-chain evidence is dispersed, but also because different parts of reasoning may require distinct, interacting chains of support. Flat retrieval can flood a model with loosely related text, whereas early selection of a single reasoning path can discard evidence that becomes useful only after other facts are considered. We introduce RESIN (Reasoning with Entity Structured Indexing), which uses structure to locate evidence while keeping the original source as the basis for reasoning. RESIN decomposes a question into focused one-hop needs, retrieves a small set of source-grounded units for each hop, and places only this relevant evidence in a compact reasoning buffer. The buffer preserves alternative supports until final composition, making intermediate decisions explicit without forcing a brittle path. On the full HolisQA benchmark, RESIN achieves 82.92 context recall and 76.70 answer relevancy over all questions, with higher reported CR and AR across five domains and higher F1 in four domains than the published baselines. Ablations show that both typed evidence diversity and hop-wise decomposition are essential, supporting a clear conclusion: Precise evidence organization and delayed commitment make multi-chain reasoning effective and auditable. Our code is available at https://github.com/RamonMeng/RESIN.

</details>

### [Beyond Attack Success Rate: Cost-Aware Metric for Evaluating Iterative LLM Jailbreaks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26589)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.26589

> Presents a cost-aware metric for evaluating LLM security, directly relevant to guardrails and safety in production systems.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Attack success rate (ASR) evaluates jailbreaks against large language models without accounting for repeated calls to the victim model. Yet each additional call gives the deployed system another opportunity to detect the attack and intervene. We introduce $\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{STEP}}$, which penalizes additional victim-model calls while retaining binary success judgments. Our evaluation considers GCG~\cite{zou2023universal}, PAIR~\cite{chao2025jailbreaking} with a step budget, and a single-step PAP~\cite{zeng2024johnny} baseline on JailBreakBench across three model families. GCG's scores decrease by 7.0--8.7 percentage points after discounting, while PAP's remain unchanged. While for PAIR~\cite{chao2025jailbreaking}, we see a wider discounting range between 3.4-12 percentage points, showing that the "step" quantity differs per iterative strategy. Reporting both metrics makes the discount due to repeated victim-model interaction explicit. Code is available \hyperlink{https://github.com/SuELS-Group/beyond_asr}{here}.

</details>

### [The Geometry of Agent Memory: Forecasting Retrieval-Bound Memory Failures, and Characterizing Its Limits](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26433)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.26433

> Analyzes memory implementation for conversational agents, highly relevant to long-term memory management.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

We study whether geometric relationships between conversation turns, memory texts, and queries can predict answer correctness before generation. We evaluate four memory implementations with dense or hybrid retrieval and one observation-summary comparator. We embed these texts in a shared space. A logistic classifier uses 25 features describing query proximity, memory-conversation correspondence, and conversation structure. On LongMemEval-small, pooled AUROC is 0.640 over 2,367 question-system pairs, below a question-difficulty baseline of 0.655. Performance varies by question type: geometry reaches 0.841 on single-session-assistant questions, but system identity alone reaches 0.826. After controlling for difficulty and identity, geometry adds no statistically established pooled improvement (delta = +0.002, 95% CI [-0.010, 0.014]). A fixed-store context-selection experiment finds higher accuracy with proximity-ranked memories than with random subsets, consistent with ordinary retrieval relevance. On LoCoMo, nearest-turn similarity has modest predictive value for single-hop questions and near-chance estimates for several other categories. Training uses correctness labels; prediction uses pre-answer features and no additional LLM generation call. The results characterize associations between memory representations and correctness, rather than establish a reliable deployment diagnostic. In particular, embedding proximity does not verify fact coverage or distinguish memory-construction, retrieval, and generation errors.

### [Large-Scale Biomedical RAG: Corpus Scaling, Document Representation, and Efficiency Trade-offs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26339)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.26339

> Directly evaluates RAG efficiency and design based on document representation.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

We present a controlled, large-scale evaluation of how corpus scale, document representation, and reranking affect the effectiveness and efficiency of biomedical Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems. Retrieval collections were progressively expanded while retaining the relevant source documents, enabling consistent comparisons across corpus sizes. We compared abstract, flat full-text, and hierarchical full-text representations across first-stage retrieval, reranking, and end-to-end multiple-choice question answering. Configurations selected on development data were evaluated on five external biomedical benchmarks and a held-out test set, while retrieval latency and index storage were measured to quantify operational cost. Performance patterns observed with small or controlled collections did not consistently persist as collections grew, and the usefulness of reranking depended on the corpus and retrieval setting. RAG improved three of four model configurations across the external benchmarks, with mean gains of up to 9.6 percentage points, and improved all four configurations on the held-out test set by 10.2-30.3 points. Abstract retrieval performed best when reference evidence was abstract-aligned, whereas full-text representations were generally more effective when richer evidence was needed. Hierarchical representation matched or outperformed flat full-text retrieval in most end-to-end comparisons while requiring 67% fewer indexed units and 44-56% less storage. These findings show that corpus scale should be treated as an explicit evaluation variable in biomedical RAG, and that the most appropriate design depends on evidence format, model behavior, and operational constraints.

### [Preference Is Not Position: Evaluating Reranking on NevIR in Fixed Hybrid Candidate Pools](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26260)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.26260

> Directly addresses reranking, a crucial aspect of retrieval evaluation in RAG systems.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Near-identical passages can differ in a decisive condition, such as negation. A ranker may order such a pair correctly while placing the preferred passage far down the retrieved list. We examine this distinction using official NevIR pairs jointly covered by fixed candidate pools from dense, learned sparse, and BM25 retrieval. On 2,736 Test query directions, a pairwise-trained ranker improves strict per-query preference over fixed fusion (56.62% versus 52.30%) while reducing preferred-passage mean reciprocal rank (MRR) from 0.5764 to 0.3055. An exploratory fixed-model replay of a variant with background sampling and revised supervision yields MRR of 0.5859 and strict preference of 54.53%. Bounded pointwise reranking of the fusion top 20 improves both outcomes. Qwen3-14B-AWQ with fusion tie-breaking reaches 77.45% strict preference, 58.99% NevIR-style paired accuracy across 1,363 complete pairs, and preferred-passage MRR of 0.7509. A BGE cross-encoder reaches 69.77% strict preference and MRR of 0.7404. A post-hoc analysis finds that the branch where equal Qwen grades for the two designated passages trigger fusion tie-breaking accounts for 27% of evaluated directions and contains most remaining strict-preference failures. These findings support joint evaluation of pairwise preference, designated-passage position, and computational cost, while distinguishing model judgments from the combined ranking procedure.

### [Benchmark for Structured Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Perovskite Photovoltaics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25575)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.25575

> Directly addresses benchmarking for RAG models in experimental materials, highly relevant for evaluation methodologies.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

While retrieval-augmented language models increasingly serve as literature assistants in the physical sciences, existing benchmarks evaluate them primarily on qualitative text overlap or through model-based scoring rather than against verified experimental ground truth. In experimental materials disciplines such as perovskite photovoltaics, however, the most critical inquiries are inherently quantitative and aggregate measurements across dozens or hundreds of papers. Here, we present a quantitative question-answering benchmark where all 755 ground-truth answers are computed directly from 45,441 measured device records (40,889 human-curated records through 2021 and 4,552 records extracted from 2022-2026 literature). Aggregate queries spanning extrema, distributions, and filtered record counts prove virtually unsolvable via text retrieval alone, demonstrating that systematic table extraction is an operational prerequisite for literature-scale quantitative inquiry.

### [When Does Evidence-Graph Structure Predict Answer Correctness? Label-Free Abstention for Structured Retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24030)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.24030

> Directly addresses evidence graph utility in retrieval systems, relevant for RAG evaluation.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieval systems return scored answers, but a high score does not show that an answer should be trusted. The proposed training-free abstention method applies the boundary cut to the selected evidence in an evidence graph. The signal is the weighted cut between selected and unselected candidates and requires no correctness labels or confidence-model training. On MemoryAgentBench (MAB) FactConsolidation multi-hop, with unresolved queries ranked last for every selector, it raises area under the precision-coverage curve from 0.736 for the best score baseline to 0.785 (+0.049, 95% CI [+0.008, +0.092]); under the native evaluation, where score baselines keep their unresolved values, the gap is +0.108. A zero-cut subset contains 71 of 400 queries at 100% observed precision. In this pipeline, the subset corresponds to structural closure; controlled analyses attribute much of the remaining separation to resolution status, with little benefit from continuous edge weights. Across external benchmarks, usefulness varies with graph construction, edge semantics, and relation; typed structure alone does not ensure useful confidence. A supervised gradient-boosted selector achieves 0.814 versus the boundary cut's 0.785 when the retrieval stack matches. These findings provide empirical guidance for using available evidence graphs for lightweight, auditable post-retrieval abstention without additional model inference.

</details>

### [One Fact, Many Places: Redundant Evidence in Long Visual Documents Breaks Single-Gold Evaluation of Retrieval, Citation and Attribution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26401)
**Score 9/10** · `cs.CV` · 2610.26401

> Directly addresses citation and retrieval issues in document understanding, very relevant for RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Document understanding is increasingly judged on where an answer comes from, scored against annotated gold locations as if each fact lived in one place. In long visual documents, a fact often lives in several places: a chart value is restated in the text, a key figure reappears in the summary. We ask how often such copies occur in standard benchmarks and what they do to evaluation. We annotate 420 questions from MMLongBench-Doc and MMDocRAG with the locations that independently establish each required fact, checked by two independent reviewers. Copies are common and often cross-modal: 19.5% of MMLongBench-Doc test questions have a strict copy outside the gold, and in 28% of MMDocRAG questions a non-gold candidate alone supplies a required fact. A model that cites a copy is scored as citing wrongly: crediting equivalent evidence reverses 11% of pairwise orderings among 65 competitive MMDocRAG systems. Masking the gold region leaves the fact available elsewhere: with a copy in the input, 41-56% of correct answers across four models survive it yet fail once every annotated instance is masked. Set-level masking with automatically found copies closes 78% of the gap to masking every annotated instance at matched granularity (three models); an unverified key-string rule lowers survival at least as far but masks three times the area. We release the annotations and redundancy-aware metrics.

### [When the harness decides success: state that cannot be re-observed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26632)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.26632

> Analyzes context management in agents, relevant to the conversational memory aspect of RAG.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Agent harnesses decide what a language model sees across turns: they drop old turns, summarize them, or replay the model's own earlier reasoning. We ask when these choices change whether an agent succeeds, not only what it costs, and report three findings. First, in a pre-registered synthetic probe, truncating the context window costs a low-cost reasoning model nothing in success when a needed fact can be looked up again (it looks the fact up instead), but drops its success to chance when the fact cannot be re-observed: the two penalties differ by 92.5 percentage points (90% CI [89.5, 95.5]). Second, on ARC-AGI-3, where the benchmark's Standard harness trails its Provider Adapter harness by a wide margin, we decompose that gap one component at a time, for one model on four games. Summarizing old turns instead of dropping them, while keeping the Standard harness's invitation to take notes, recovers 86% of a 63-point gap without replaying the model's reasoning. The same harness without the invitation recovers 43%, and adding the provider's native replay of the model's reasoning recovers the remaining 57%. These are point estimates from one counted run per configuration and game, and the replay share depends on a single run. Third, in the benchmark's published runs and in ours, the Adapter's advantage in the probability of clearing a level it has reached is larger at deep levels than at early ones. This largely restates where runs stopped and does not separate a growing advantage from a constant one.

### [Budgeted Attack Scheduling for LLM Red-Teaming: The Roles of Static Priors, Semantic Guidance, and Online Feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25897)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.CL` · 2610.25897

> Discusses jailbreak attacks and robustness evaluation, relevant to safety for LLM applications.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

As jailbreak attacks against large language models (LLMs) become increasingly diverse, red-teaming has come to rely on large libraries of jailbreak prompts and attack strategies to evaluate the robustness of victim models and their defense settings. However, attack effectiveness is highly condition-dependent: an attack that succeeds under one victim-defense setting may fail under another, while exhaustively evaluating the full attack library for each setting incurs substantial token and monetary costs. This motivates studying how jailbreak attacks should be prioritized under a limited attempt budget for efficient robustness evaluation. Extensive experiments clearly characterize the conditions under which each signal is most effective. Historical ASR provides a strong initialization, but its reliability degrades under defense-induced distribution shifts. Semantic guidance consistently improves or matches static prior-based scheduling in the cold-start regime, while online feedback becomes particularly valuable when transferred priors become stale. Semantic guidance and online feedback can also partially compensate for missing historical priors. Taken together, these findings characterize budgeted red-teaming as an attack scheduling problem in which historical priors, semantic guidance, and online feedback play complementary roles.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Long-running agents rely on memory of their past interactions for task state, such as what has been completed and which conditions still hold. Such state is usually a derived state. No record states it, and it must be computed by rules from multiple records of an evolving history. Existing memory benchmarks score only the final answer, with no reference for the intermediate states behind it. A wrong answer therefore cannot be traced to the state that was missed, and a state that silently became outdated goes undetected. We present MemDerive, a diagnostic benchmark for agent memory over evolving data. It specifies each scenario symbolically, derives exact gold answers with Datalog, records the gold derivation DAG and the supporting events of every query, and renders the history in natural language with fictional entities and distractors. Its 10 scenarios contain 4,692 events and 1,503 queries whose difficulty varies along access, evolution, and composition. On this ground truth, MemDerive evaluates answer reliability, diagnoses failures by probing the nodes of the gold derivation DAG, and tracks whether answers stay correct as events arrive. Our evaluation of four memory systems reveals failures that final-answer benchmarks hide. On derived state, every memory system falls behind simple BM25 retrieval, although the same answer model reaches 96.01% when given the supporting events. Most failures are ungathered, as a system holds the missing intermediate state but does not retrieve it for the question that needs it. Memory systems also rarely propagate implicit changes, which no new record states. These results suggest three principles for memory design. Gather inputs along the derivation, store rules with their versions, and maintain derived states through recorded dependencies.

### [Learning to Program Retrievals: Composing Search Primitives via Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24854)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.24854

> Discusses programmable retrieval mechanisms which are relevant for enhancing RAG strategies.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Retrieval-augmented language models increasingly use multi-step search to solve knowledge-intensive tasks, yet existing systems typically expose retrieval through fixed pipelines or collections of atomic tools. We introduce Retrieval Program Learning (RPL), a programmable search framework that allows a policy to compose search primitives into executable programs with local state and control flow. This enables adaptive search that can retrieve broadly, process intermediate results, and selectively construct model context without tying retrieval breadth to context consumption. However, the resulting flexibility creates a difficult exploration problem: useful programs may require several coordinated decisions, while reinforcement learning often supplies only sparse feedback. To address this challenge, we propose program mutation, a grammar-constrained and execution-verified procedure that generates local variants of suboptimal programs sampled during RL, converts them into training trajectories, and distills them back into the policy. We evaluate RPL on open-domain question answering and corpus-level reasoning tasks, comparing it with a range of retrieval and reasoning baselines. RPL consistently improves open-domain QA performance across model sizes and in- and out-of-domain benchmarks, with particularly large gains on multi-hop tasks. Notably, a 4B model trained with RPL outperforms all 8B model baselines. On corpus-level reasoning, RPL achieves the highest answer F1 and document-coverage F1 for both model sizes while substantially reducing context consumption and retrieval turns. Our results show that programmable search expands the retrieval strategies that agents can learn, while program mutation provides an effective mechanism for exploring useful program structures beyond direct on-policy sampling.

### [MemRevise: A Self-Revising Memory for Conversational Search](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24672)
**Score 8/10** · `cs.IR` · 2610.24672

> Relevant to conversational memory with a focus on memory dynamics in conversational search.

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

In conversational search, users correct misun-derstandings, revise their requests and return to earlier topics, so part of the history no longer reflects what they want. Existing benchmarks do not isolate such turns. Current search methods either re-read the history or keep a record of every turn, and none of them marks earlier content as dropped. We introduce CONVREVISE, a benchmark of 4,429 test questions in six tiers of returns and revisions over TopiOCQA, CORAL and OR-QuAC. We also propose MEMREVISE, which combines a revisable memory graph, a 3B writer distilled from teacher memories, and a search guided by the memory. The writer reads each turn once and adds a node that records its topic, its facts and the operations it performs, such as adding, abandoning or reactivating a topic. Topic status follows from these operations, so a dropped topic keeps its facts and can be restored. With a frozen retriever, MEMREVISE reaches a mean MRR@10 of 0.701, against 0.589 for the best competitor, ConvSearch-R1-FT, and 0.617 for a writer distilled the same way without the memory, so the memory and the steps built on it add 0.084. MEMREVISE is significantly better on Topi- OCQA and OR-QuAC, statistically tied with prompted 32B rewriters on CORAL, and never significantly behind the strongest competitor of any tier.
